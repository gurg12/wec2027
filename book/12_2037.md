# 2037 — Cognition Too Cheap to Meter

*Akron, Ohio. February 3, 2037.*

April Sandoval's household budget, which she keeps in a spiral notebook out of loyalty to her late mother's method, has become a document her mother would not recognize. On the left page, the things that got cheap: her son's tutor — better than the one the private school had when she was cleaning its offices — $4 a month; the family's medical triage, diagnostics, and most of its therapy, bundled free with the state exchange plan; legal help for her sister's custody filing, free at the library kiosk, better than the $3,000 lawyer from 2029; tax prep, translation for her mother-in-law, the drafting of the grant application that funded her co-op — free, free, free. On the right page, the things that did the opposite: rent on the two-bedroom, up 9% again; the used truck; her share of the co-op's building, the old Methodist church on Grace Avenue they bought before the neighborhood noticed itself. April runs what she calls a *gathering business* — grief circles on Tuesdays, a men's choir on Thursdays, job-transition coaching funded by AWTA vouchers, quinceañera and funeral planning on the weekends. Everything her clients need to *know* costs nothing now. What they pay her for is to be *held to it* — to show up, to be seen showing up, to have someone notice when they don't. Her accountant — a resident agent her son configured, naturally, for free — categorized her business last April as "personal services, other." She has crossed it out on the printout and written what she actually sells, in her mother's blue ink: *company.*

---

**In January**, the number becomes official enough to argue with: the year's pricing surveys put inference at the 2026 frontier's capability level at **two cents per million tokens** — effectively free at the point of use, five orders of magnitude below its debut cost, bundled now into phone plans and library cards the way voice minutes once were.¹ **[HIGH]** The container-shipping analogy the spine promised is the one the economists actually use, because the numbers rhyme: Malcom McLean's box cut ocean freight costs by ~90% and redrew where everything was made; the cognition cost-curve cut the price of a competent analyst-hour by ~99% in a decade and is redrawing where everything is *decided*.² What container shipping did to goods — dissolved distance as a cost, then reorganized the world around the remaining frictions — the token curve does to knowledge work: dissolves *expertise* as a cost, then reorganizes everything around what still can't ship, which turns out to be trust, presence, responsibility, and taste. April Sandoval's notebook is the household version of the national accounts: **services deflation against asset inflation**, everything you *do* getting cheaper, everything you *own* getting dearer — the defining economic experience of the decade, and the quiet driver of politics everywhere, because a world where knowledge is free and shelter is not is a world that has relocated scarcity, not abolished it.³ **[HIGH]**

**In March**, the labor statisticians confirm the shape April lives: the **purpose economy** graduates from feuilleton to dataset. The BLS's occupational projections put care choreography, coaching and facilitation, craft production, religious and ritual services, and live performance atop the growth table for the third straight year — the fastest-growing employment categories in every rich economy, now 11% of US employment and climbing a point a year.⁴ **[MED]** The columnists split predictably: half see a renaissance of the human, half see a servant economy with better branding, and the data supports both — median wages in the category run 12% below the national median (presence is abundant labor), while its top decile (the celebrants, the coaches with waitlists, the verified-human performers of the post-VERA premium tier) out-earns the median lawyer of 2026. The honest summary, which this book files for the 2041 chapter's darker sequel: work is *relocating into meaning* faster than meaning can be made to pay.

**In October**, Beijing opens the window everyone has watched for a decade. At the 23rd Party Congress, Xi Jinping — 84 now, the country's demographic pivot his last great campaign — retains the Central Military Commission but hands the General Secretaryship to **Zhao Minhui**, 61, an engineer-administrator who ran Guangdong's automation decade, alongside a Politburo Standing Committee deliberately rebuilt at seven seats with two visible factions — the choreography of *collective leadership, restored in miniature*.⁵ **[MED]** The tea-leaf industry declares everything from restoration to regency, and the truth, this book wagers, is the boring middle: a managed, staged transition in which the succession's real electorate — the security organs, the provincial machines, and now the demographic-industrial complex Wang Jun's order book answers to — has agreed on continuity's *terms* while the founder is alive to bless them. Growth prints 2.4%; the robotics-as-pension strategy enters its second five-year plan; and the signal Washington actually files: Zhao's first foreign-policy sentence reaffirms, verbatim, the accords, the compact talks (Chapter 13 is five months away), and "a stable strait for a stable century." Continuity as product. Markets, which have long since learned this book's lesson that boring is the bull case, add half a multiple.

**In June**, between the giant beats, the machinery of ordinary life keeps re-pricing itself in ways the century will remember longer than the summits. Three entries from the year's diffusion ledger, chosen because each was impossible the decade before: a Nairobi teaching hospital runs its residency program with case-simulation faculty that would have cost a medical school's endowment in 2026, free now, and matches Johns Hopkins on board scores; the Mexican social-security system clears its infamous specialist backlog — 14 months to 11 days — by routing every referral through diagnostic triage that outperforms the panels it replaced (the 2031 clearances, compounding); and in Osaka, the first **care-choreography deficit** makes the news — robots idle in a ward because the humans licensed to supervise them are the scarce input now, Marisol's profession having become, in fifteen years, the bottleneck of the aging world.⁶ **[MED texture, HIGH direction]** Cognition too cheap to meter, the engineers' old joke about electricity finally lands on its true referent — and, as with electricity, the meter turns out to have been the point: what you stop metering, you start assuming, and what you assume, you build *everything* on.

**In December**, the year closes the way transition years do, with the old scarcity's obituary buried inside the new one's birth announcement. Global growth prints 3.2% — the fifth straight year above three, the best half-decade since the 2000s, productivity's long wave now visibly global — while the rich world's political mood, measured by every index available, is *sour*: the asset side of April's notebook is winning, homeownership ages keep rising, and the treated-untreated split of 2036 adds a biological floor to the era's status anxieties.⁷ Gold ends at $8,540; the 10-year real yield at 0.3%; oil at $43, now just a commodity among commodities; and the phrase of the year, coined by a Seoul columnist and translated everywhere within the month, names the condition this book has been circling since the four-day week arrived: *풍요 속의 갈증 — thirst amid plenty.* The purpose economy is the market's answer to it. The 2041 chapter is what happens when the market's answer isn't enough.
