# 2029 — The Fiscal Turn

*Alcalá de Henares, Spain. June 13, 2029.*

The residents of the Santa Lucía eldercare home do not follow bond markets, with the exception of Sr. Ibarra, ninety-one, who follows everything. Marisol Ferreyra — care systems coordinator now, badge and everything — finds him at breakfast with two newspapers and the tablet the home issued him, explaining to a table of skeptical ninety-year-olds that France could not sell its debt yesterday. "Not *could not*," he corrects himself, professorial. "*Chose not to, at that price.* There is always a price." The table wants to know if their pensions are French. Marisol spends the morning doing what her job actually consists of — being the human interface between institutional systems and human fear — pulling up the fund statements with each resident who asks, showing them the boring Spanish and European lines. What she doesn't say: her own mother in Rosario has lived through six of these — the corralito, the defaults, the word "haircut" arriving in Spanish — and the family reflex is already activated; her mother sent a voice note at 6 a.m. asking whether Marisol's euros were "in something real." Argentines, Marisol thinks, are not better economists than anyone else. They are just further along the syllabus. At lunch, Sr. Ibarra asks her, privately, to help him buy a little gold on his tablet, "not because I am afraid — because I am *experienced*." She helps him. It takes four minutes. The app has a waiting list notification she has never seen before.

---

**In January**, Dana Whitfield is inaugurated behind bulletproof glass and in front of a teleprompter she pointedly turns off for four minutes, and the machine age gets its first American government elected specifically to referee it. The First Hundred Days list is long, but the year will be remembered for three structures, none of which was the loudest thing announced: the bifurcation, the backstop, and the levy fight.

**In March**, the bifurcation becomes law, and the AI backlash era — dated by this book from the Gallup collapse of 2026 — finds its peace treaty. **[HIGH]** The **Consumer AI Standards Act** passes 71–28: labeling of synthetic media and AI interlocutors, strict liability for AI products sold to consumers, age-gating for companion and tutoring products, a federal provenance mark. Its unglamorous twin, negotiated in the same committee and reported by almost nobody, is what the act does *not* cover: enterprise, infrastructure, and research systems — the resident agents, the routing layers, the lab loops — which get audit requirements modeled on banking supervision and otherwise a green light.¹ Brussels lands within a hundred days of Washington with its AI Act revision on the same chassis. The politics are explicit, if you listened: the public's fear was of the AI it could *see* — the fake voices, the chatbot in the classroom, the slop. What it could see gets labeled, gated, and suable. What it couldn't see gets a supervisor and a growth path.

> **SIDEBAR: The Bifurcation.** Every durable technology settlement splits the visible from the structural: aviation regulates the passenger cabin ferociously and the freight networks technically; pharma splits over-the-counter from prescription. The 2029 AI settlement does the same along the axis of *perceptibility*. Consumer AI — anything that talks to, depicts, or teaches a human — becomes among the most regulated product categories in American law. Infrastructure AI — anything that talks to software — becomes a supervised utility layer, treated like clearing houses: audited, stress-tested, and otherwise left to compound. Critics on both flanks call it surrender. Its defense is the next twenty years, in which it holds. The unpriced consequence: the economy's cognitive machinery becomes *invisible by statute*, and a polity that regulates what it sees slowly loses sight of what it runs on.² That bill arrives in 2034. **[HIGH on the settlement; the 2034 linkage is the author's argument]**

**In June**, France. **[MED]** The proximate cause is almost insultingly ordinary — a pension re-reform bill dies in a hung Assembly for the third time, the 2030 budget slips to a 6.1% deficit, and a rating agency moves on a Thursday — but the mechanism is the one this book has been tracking since the Warsh hold of 2026: a developed world issuing paper faster than unforced buyers arrive. The OAT-Bund spread, 90 basis points in January, is 195 by early June; on **June 12** a routine ten-year auction covers at 1.4× only because the primary dealers are leaned on, and everyone can see the lean. Money-market funds begin treating French collateral like Italian. On **June 19**, after a weekend of the kind that produces communiqués timed for Asia's open, the ECB announces the **Market Continuity Instrument**: open-ended purchases of member sovereign debt "to preserve the transmission of monetary policy," conditionality described in a footnote and never invoked.³ No target yield is announced. A corridor emerges anyway within weeks — traders christen it *yield-curve control in a trench coat* — and OATs stabilize with the ten-year pinned near 3.9%. The taboo breaks in October, at a Frankfurt conference, when a Governing Council member says the sentence out loud that a decade of central bankers had died rather than utter: *"Where fiscal positions constrain monetary choices, our duty is to make that constraint survivable — some call this fiscal dominance; I call it arithmetic."*⁴ The euro falls 4% that quarter. Gold, already re-bid since the auction, ends the year at $6,410. The Argentine syllabus, as Marisol's mother could have explained, has a name for the whole sequence, and the name is *chapter one*.

**In August**, the levy fight ends in the draw that becomes the American template: Whitfield's compute levy passes the House, dies in the Senate by two, and is reborn by executive-adjacent means — a negotiated framework in which the hyperscalers "voluntarily" fund $18 billion a year of grid buildout and ratepayer rebates in exchange for federal siting preemption in participating states.⁵ **[MED]** Six governors sign on by Christmas; electricity-bill politics cools a degree; the buildout, which never stopped, gets faster and more consolidated. The clean loser is the open question of *worker* compensation — wage insurance dies with the levy — and the missing-rung cohort notices. That fight is postponed, not settled; it returns in 2032 with compound interest.

**In October**, two quiet firsts, one atoms and one biology. In Phoenix — the oldest robotaxi market on Earth, eight years deep — autonomous vehicles cross **50% of ride-hail miles** for a full quarter, the first city where the steering wheel is the minority interface for paid rides.⁶ **[MED]** The same month, **MRB-104** — an idiopathic-fibrosis therapy whose target hypothesis, molecular design, *and* trial architecture were generated end-to-end by Meridian Bio's discovery stack — enters Phase III, the first candidate with no human-authored step between disease and dose.⁷ **[MED]** Neither event trends. Both are load-bearing: the Phoenix statistic is the first proof that robotics' 5-to-7-year lag behind cognition ends in arrival rather than asymptote, and MRB-104 starts the clock on the medical results that will, in 2031, begin thawing a public that labeling laws only quieted.

**In November**, Russia begins discussing the succession in the way Russia discusses things — through appointments. **[HIGH]** Putin, 77 now, appears less and delegates more visibly; the autumn reshuffle elevates **Dmitri Kolesnikov**, 54, a career security-services economist who spent the war years running the sanctions-adaptation apparatus, to First Deputy Prime Minister with a portfolio drawn like a regency — finance, digital infrastructure, and the internal troops' procurement.⁸ Nobody says heir. Everybody counts the portfolio. The liberals-in-exile debate boycotts of an election nobody has scheduled; the men who matter — the service factions this book will call, following the Moscow joke, *the electorate* — begin negotiating with each other, carefully, through arrests at the margins of each other's networks. The ceasefire line, two years old this month, holds through all of it: whatever comes next in Moscow, no faction's plan requires the front to reopen. Reconstruction Kyiv, meanwhile, posts the EU's fastest construction-sector growth for the second straight year, a sentence that would have read as satire in 2024.⁹

**In December**, the year closes on an American labor market that has stopped deteriorating and started *rearranging*: unemployment steady at 4.7%, quits low, part-time-for-economic-reasons elevated, and — the number that will define the coming decade's politics — prime-age male detachment at a modern high even as measured output per hour begins, at last, to twitch upward in the national accounts' fine print.¹⁰ The productivity statisticians publish a working paper in December titled *"Early Evidence of General-Purpose Technology Effects in Services Output"* and it is exactly as cautious as its title, and it is the most important boring document of the year. The lag the historians promised — dynamo to assembly line, decades compressed to years — is ending on schedule. 2030 gets the headline. **[MED]**
