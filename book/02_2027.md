# 2027 — The Capex Reckoning

*Madrid, Spain. March 26, 2027.*

Marisol Ferreyra learns she has been laid off from a push notification that arrives before the meeting does. She is thirty-two, from Rosario originally, six years into a Madrid banking job that her parents in Argentina still describe to neighbors as "working in finance, in Europe," as if it were a diplomatic posting. She is — was — a junior analyst on a team that builds valuation models for infrastructure deals, and the infrastructure deals of the last two years have overwhelmingly been data centers, and the models, as of this quarter, build themselves. The notification says her division is being "right-sized in light of market conditions and productivity improvements," a phrase that manages to blame both the crash and the thing that caused her job to be automatable in the same breath. In the lobby, security is polite. The strange part, she will tell her sister that night, is that the bank isn't doing badly — the machines that replaced her team cost almost nothing to run now, which is precisely the problem for whoever sold the bank its compute. On the Metro home she opens her banking app, looks at fourteen months of runway if she's careful, and does something she has never done: she writes down, on paper, a list titled *things I can do that a model can't.* It is a short list. It is not empty. Item four, underlined twice, says: *show up in a room.*

---

**In January**, the market's largest-ever concentration trade meets its first real earnings test and fails it politely. **[MED]** The fourth-quarter reports are not disasters; they are *disappointments with excellent production values* — hyperscaler capex guidance for 2027 comes in at $780 billion against the trillion the sell side had penciled in, two of the big four use the word "digestion," and a neocloud called **Cardinal Compute**, the third-largest GPU landlord in North America, discloses that 40% of its contracted revenue sits with counterparties whose own funding closed after the music stopped.¹ That is all it takes. The index of AI-concentrated names peaks on January 21 and gives back 31% by the June trough; the S&P, hostage to eleven tickers, drops 19% peak-to-trough; Cardinal files for Chapter 11 in May and its data centers change hands at 55 cents on the dollar without a single server powering down.² The comparison everyone reaches for is 2008. The right one is 2000: an equity fantasy dying while the underlying traffic curve — this is the part nobody prices — *never has a down month.*³ Token consumption across the industry roughly quadruples during the crash itself, because the marginal token now costs a fifth of what it did eighteen months ago, and at those prices the enterprise layer stops experimenting and starts *routing*.⁴

> **SIDEBAR: 2000, not 2008.** Why a $2-trillion drawdown produced no financial crisis: the buildout was funded the old-fashioned way — retained earnings and investment-grade debt at companies with fortress balance sheets, plus equity at the fringe. There was no 30:1 bank leverage under it, no repo chain, no money-market run. When it cracked, shareholders ate it, as shareholders did in 2000. The dot-com precedent runs deeper: the fiber laid in 1999 at fantasy valuations carried the real internet of 2005 at bankruptcy prices. The GPUs of 2026 will spend 2028 doing exactly that. Overbuilding is how infrastructure gets cheap enough to matter; the reckoning is the discount mechanism. **[HIGH]**

**In February**, the Fed finally moves — down. With headline CPI at 2.9% (oil in the fifties does faster work than any committee) and the labor market shedding jobs at the white-collar margin, Warsh delivers the first of three cuts that will land by July, taking the funds rate to 2.75–3.00%.⁵ He frames each one as recalibration rather than rescue, and pointedly declines to mention equity prices in a single statement all spring — a silence Wall Street reads, correctly, as doctrine. The bond market's verdict is stranger: the 10-year barely rallies through the whole drawdown, pinned near 4% by supply. A trillion-dollar-deficit country cutting rates into 3% inflation is doing something with a name, and by autumn the name is on research covers: *the fiscal put has replaced the Fed put.* Gold, which entered the year at $4,680, does not correct with the equity market this time. It ends the year at $5,150. **[MED]**

**In April**, the other shoe drops in Mandarin. Zhipu releases **GLM-6** with open weights a week after its benchmark debut, and by summer the third-party evals agree on the number that matters: for roughly 90% of enumerable enterprise tasks — extraction, reconciliation, first-draft code, tier-two support, the valuation models Marisol used to build — the open Chinese frontier is indistinguishable from the closed American one at one-sixth to one-tenth the price.⁶ **[HIGH]** Inference prices, already falling, collapse: the blended cost of a million tokens drops ~80% year over year. The US labs keep the true frontier — the long-horizon agents, the lab-grade reasoning — but the *commodity* layer of intelligence is now priced in Hangzhou. Two consequences arrive immediately: every enterprise procurement conversation on Earth gains a second bidder, and every Washington argument about chip export controls quietly shifts from "keep them behind" to "keep the frontier," which everyone involved understands to be a smaller thing to keep.

**In June**, the graduating class of 2027 walks into the statistic. **[HIGH]** The BLS data stops being deniable mid-year: unemployment among degree-holders aged 22–27 crosses above the national rate for the first time in the modern series; postings tagged entry-level analyst, associate, or paralegal run 44% below 2024; the big consultancies and banks take graduating classes a third the size of 2023's.⁷ The displacement is not mass unemployment — the overall rate peaks at 4.9% — it is a *missing rung*. The juniors aren't fired; they're never hired. Editorials note, with varying degrees of glee, that the class of 2027 is the most credentialed cohort in history and the first to be told the credential's first job doesn't exist. What the columns miss is who these people are: articulate, online, organized, and now available. The anti-AI politics of 2026 had homeowners and electric bills. As of this summer it has cadres.

**In August**, an internet measurement consortium publishes the crossing: automated agents now generate a majority of traffic on the public internet — 53% and climbing, up from the high-40s bot share that had held for years, the increment being not scrapers but *transacting* agents: negotiating, purchasing, filing, scheduling.⁸ **[HIGH]** The milestone gets one news cycle and no policy response. It will get the policy response in 2034. A niche standards effort — signed capture, human-attestation tiers — adds a few engineers. Machine traffic passing human traffic is the kind of threshold civilizations notice only in retrospect, which is why this book is flagging it now.

**In November**, the war in Ukraine stops — not ends, stops. **[MED]** The sequence is exhaustion arithmetic, not diplomacy: Russia enters the autumn with its liquid reserve fund spent, refining still 15% impaired, and a draft class it cannot politically enlarge; Ukraine enters it with Europe's €90 billion flowing but a manpower deficit no money fixes.⁹ In September, Russian offensive operations around Kostiantynivka simply taper — ammunition rationing visible from satellite. In October, back-channels in Doha (the annex city has become the decade's Geneva) produce a document nobody signs at a table: a **ceasefire-in-place**, effective November 11, monitored by satellites and a UN technical mission, security guarantees "fudged" into a lattice — a European reassurance force west of the Dnipro on Ukrainian invitation, a US intelligence backstop renewed annually, no NATO membership and no recognition of any Russian annexation.¹⁰ The line freezes roughly where it has stood since winter. Nobody celebrates. Kyiv's bond spreads tighten 400 basis points in a week, and the reconstruction consortia — German, Polish, Turkish, Korean — begin letting contracts by December. Moscow declares victory, cuts the war tax it had just raised, and turns to the only conflict that now matters there: Putin turned 75 in October, and the men around him have begun, discreetly, to campaign for a job that is not vacant. **[HIGH]**

**All year, in the background**, the cold peace with Iran holds and — this is the annex's real achievement — *bores* markets. The 120-day extension rolls into a 180-day framework in December 2026, renewing on schedule thereafter; a sanctions tranche unlocks in June; Hormuz transit insurance premia end the year below their pre-war average, because the strait now carries the one guarantee that matters, which is that everyone has seen what closing it costs. Mojtaba Khamenei appears in public for the first time in September — seated, briefly, at a Qom seminary event, face partially averted from the cameras — and the theocracy's succession question settles into the same shape as Russia's: not *whether* the old system continues, but *which faction of its security services inherits it*.¹¹ Oil averages $56 for the year. The strait that owned 2026 has become, eighteen months later, a utility.

*Madrid, December 19.* Marisol has not found a job in finance. She has found, instead, a contract — through her aunt, a geriatric nurse — helping an eldercare company in Alcalá de Henares evaluate scheduling software, which turns out to mean explaining to engineers what a night shift actually is. It pays 60% of the old salary. On Fridays she takes the certification course the wage-subsidy office funds, in something called "care systems coordination," a phrase she would have laughed at two years ago. Item four on her paper list — *show up in a room* — has, to her genuine surprise, become her profession. The last class before Christmas covers fall-detection sensors. She raises her hand and asks what the sensor does when the resident is frightened rather than fallen. The instructor checks the documentation. The documentation doesn't say. *Write that down,* says the instructor, and she does, and for the first time in nine months Marisol has the feeling of being early to something rather than late.
