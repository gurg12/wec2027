# 2032 — The New Deal for Work

*Riyadh, Saudi Arabia. January 20, 2032.*

Reem al-Otaibi is twenty-nine, has a master's in petroleum economics she has never professionally used, and spends her days running load forecasts for a campus that consumes more electricity than the city she was born in. She joined the sovereign fund's infrastructure arm out of university expecting to model oil demand; instead she models *cooling* — the great constraint of the kingdom's second act, in which the export is no longer hydrocarbons but the work they can be turned into before they ever leave. Her campus — NEOM North, though nobody calls it that; the engineers call it "the block" — sells training-hours to three continents, timesharing between a Korean chaebol's foundation models, an Indian tutoring platform's overnight runs, and, as of December, the European Central Bank's stress-testing sandbox, a contract her team celebrated with cake because irony is a food group here now. Her grandfather herded near Ha'il; her father flew for Aramco; she watches megawatts. On her screen this morning: solar noon in an hour, the block's price per petaflop-hour about to drop 31% as the desert does what the desert is now *for*. Her cousin texts from Jeddah — he's applying to the tourism academy, third year it's oversubscribed — asking if she thinks the future has room for tour guides. She looks at the booking queue from Frankfurt, from Mumbai, from Seoul, all of them buying Saudi sunlight transubstantiated into cognition, and types: *Habibi, the future is entirely made of guides.*

---

**In March**, the bill that died in 2029 comes back with a coalition that couldn't have existed then, and passes. The **American Work Transition Act** is not UBI — every sponsor says so in every interview, with the discipline of people who have read the same polling — it is *transition insurance*: displaced workers receive 50% of the gap between old and new wages for up to three years, retraining accounts travel with the worker, and firms above five thousand employees must maintain apprenticeship ratios — a hard floor of first-rung jobs, priced as a cost of doing business at scale, explicitly modeled on how societies already handle trade adjustment and disaster.¹ **[MED]** The funding is the 2028 campaign's ghost made law: the compute-levy framework, renegotiated from "voluntary" to statutory at 2.2% of covered compute revenue. What changed since 2029 is arithmetic and biography: the productivity windfall made the money real, and the missing-rung cohort — median age now 27, voting like a class since 2028 — supplied both the outrage and, in a detail the historians will enjoy, eleven of the House staffers who drafted the text. Brussels lands its **Transition Insurance Directive** in October, same chassis, more paperwork. Neither passes anywhere else. The middle-income world watches, takes notes, and mostly cannot afford the premium — which becomes its own geopolitics by 2040.

**In June**, the statistical agencies confirm what the parking lots already knew: the four-day week is the white-collar norm — 54% of US professional workers on formal four-day schedules, from 9% in 2027 — and it arrived without a single law being passed.² **[HIGH]** The mechanism was competition, not compassion: once resident agents absorbed the compilation-and-coordination layer of office work, the fifth day became the cheapest benefit in the compensation menu, and the first movers in each sector set the market. The productivity data refuses to punish it; output per worker *rises* in the transition cohorts. The politics are quietly enormous — the labor movement that spent a century fighting for hours won this round without leading it, a fact its own historians will argue about — and the cultural surface changes texture: Thursday is the new Friday, church and league attendance tick up for the first time in decades, and an entire genre of commentary is born asking what Americans will do with fifty-two found days a year. (The honest answer, per the time-use surveys: care work, second jobs, and screens — in that order, and the ordering is the story the 2041 chapter is about.)

**In August**, the Middle East completes the pivot it spent six years sequencing. Saudi–Israel normalization signs in August — embassies, direct flights, a defense-technology annex, and a Palestinian provision engineered to be simultaneously indispensable and unenforceable — the capstone on an arc that ran Hormuz, memorandum, annex, reintegration.³ **[MED]** Iran, eight tranches deep into sanctions relief, is by year's end the strangest node in the region's new economy: its oil flows openly, its engineers staff the Gulf's compute corridor, its internal politics remain a sealed regency around a supreme leader whose public appearances can still be counted on two hands. The Gulf's own transformation is the year's structural fact: the GCC states now earn more from *non-oil* rents — compute, logistics, finance, tourism, the Hajj-to-holiday complex Reem's cousin is banking on — than from crude in two of six members, with the rest converging.⁴ **[HIGH]** The oil age is not ending there; it is being *laundered into its successors*, barrel by barrel, exactly as the sovereign funds' 2026 annual reports said it would be, back when everyone read those documents as marketing.

**In November**, Whitfield wins the election that was always going to be a referendum on whether the windfall was real: 50.9%, 289 electoral votes, against a Republican campaign built on sovereignty, the border, and the accusation — not wrong, just early — that the transition state is a new entitlement wearing work boots.⁵ **[MED — the direction: incumbency riding measured productivity, not the name]** The realignment's texture is in the crosstabs: she loses ground with the over-65s (who read the AWTA as someone else's insurance) and holds the under-35s by 19, and the county map now correlates better with *data-center proximity* than with the old urban-rural gradient — pro-incumbent where the rebates flowed, anti- where the substations rose and the rebates didn't.

*Alcalá de Henares, December 9.* Marisol Ferreyra's Fridays are hers now — the home went four-day for coordination staff in March, chasing a competitor's job listing — and she has spent forty of the found Fridays this year on the course that will, next spring, make her a licensed fleet supervisor: the person legally accountable for eight care robots and the eleven humans they work between.⁶ The directive's ink is three weeks old and her sector is already its test case. On the last Friday of the year she takes Sr. Ibarra — ninety-four now, still solvent, still smug about his gold — to the notary to witness his will. He has left his tablet to the home's library and a letter to "the coordinator," to be opened professionally, upon need. She does not cry in front of him. He would deduct points. On the bus back he watches the robots unloading groceries at the service door and delivers the year's verdict, unsolicited, as always: "In my century, machines took the work and kept the money. In yours, they take the work and — sometimes, if you vote correctly — *mail some of it back*. This is called progress, niña. It is slower than the brochure."
