# 2054 — The Last Generation to Die on Schedule

*Chicago, Illinois. November 3, 2054.*

Marcus Reyes-Ward, fifty-five, writes obituaries for the *Tribune* group — one of nine humans left who do, verified-human tier, waiting list of months, because the families who can pay have learned what the machine drafts miss: not the facts, the *weight*. This morning's file: Eleanor Vasquez, 97, machinist's daughter, ER nurse for forty years, untreated — by choice, her son emphasizes, faith and stubbornness in equal parts — dead "on schedule," as the terrible new idiom has it, while her treated twin sister, biological seventy-something, survives her looking twenty years her junior. Marcus interviews the sister for an hour and writes down the sentence he knows the machines could generate and could never *mean*, the sentence his whole strange profession exists to witness: "I got the time we always wanted, and she got the life we always had, and I am no longer sure who was cheated." **[The chapter's thesis, in one grief]**

---

**In June**, the statistics catch up with the idiom: median age at death in the leading rich countries reaches **95** — up from 81 at this book's opening — with the distribution's shape transformed as Chapter 17 promised: the long plateau, the brief window, morbidity compressed into the final eighteen months for treated cohorts.¹ **[MED]** And the discourse names the cohort now in middle age — Marcus's cohort, Tomek's, the treated fifty-somethings whose parents are dying at 95 and whose own actuarial tables trail off into methodological footnotes — **"the last generation to die on schedule."** The phrase is premature, as such phrases always are, and this book says so with its full chest: no approved therapy extends maximum lifespan; the oldest verified human remains within reach of Calment's 122; the mortality curve past 100 has flattened but not folded; "schedules" have merely widened.² But the phrase is *load-bearing culturally*, exactly as the spine specified — it reorganizes retirement, inheritance (the great wealth transfer now arriving a full generation late, the 2050s' quietest economic fact), religion's oldest product line, and the marriage vow's actuarial fine print — and this book's standing rule applies one last time: what a civilization believes about its trajectory governs it as surely as the trajectory does.³ Between the treated sister and the untreated twin, between the schedule and the plateau, the century's oldest inequality — time itself — has become negotiable, purchasable, and therefore, for the first time in the species' history, *political*. The 2060s will legislate it. This book, its own schedule nearly run, leaves the gate flagged and the reader warned.
