# 2045 — The Midcentury Settlement

*Riyadh, Saudi Arabia. October 2, 2045.*

Reem al-Otaibi, forty-two, deputy governor now of the fund she joined to model cooling loads, keeps three clocks on her office wall — Washington, Beijing, and a third face her predecessor installed as a joke that stopped being one: *Everyone Else*, set to Riyadh time. Her week explains the wall. Monday, the American bloc's trade delegation, wanting the fund's compute corridor inside their attestation umbrella on preferential terms. Wednesday, the Chinese, wanting the same corridor's *southern* expansion, financed in yuan, staffed by their idle contractors. Thursday — the day that matters — the swing bloc's own finance ministers' video-call: India, Nigeria, Indonesia, Brazil, the Gulf, forty minutes, no communiqué, in which the real business of the mid-century gets done: pricing the two suitors against each other and settling, quietly, what *not* to let either buy. Her grandfather herded; her father flew; Reem *arbitrages* — that is the family trajectory, and it is also, she would say without embarrassment, her civilization's. On Friday she takes her daughter to the launch viewing at the science center: the kingdom's third lunar-water tender rides up on the afternoon window, and the girl, nine, asks the question of the age as the contrail feathers: "Mama, whose side is the Moon on?" Reem gives her the answer the third clock taught her, the one this book has been earning for twenty years: "The Moon is non-aligned, habibti. Like us. That's why everyone calls, and nobody commands."

---

**In May**, the shape of the world gets its statistics, and the statistics get their name. The IMF's mid-century review — the quota reform finally lands this year, a decade late and therefore on time — publishes the tripolar-plus arithmetic as its base framework: a US-led maritime-democratic bloc at roughly 34% of global output; a China-led continental bloc at 27%; and the genuinely non-aligned swing — India, most of Africa, the Gulf, half of ASEAN and Latin America — at 31% and *rising*, the only share that is.¹ **[HIGH]** The order's texture, mid-century: great-power war did not happen — eighty years of the Long Peace now a hundred, the streak extended through Hormuz, the drill, Brazil, and every February since by the same three disciplines (deterrence, dependence, and the newer one, *attested transparency*) — and the violence that did happen ran exactly where the spine routed it: civil wars, cyber events, climate displacement, chokepoint coercion, sanctions as siege. The Sahel's decade of collapse-and-reconstitution killed more people than every interstate incident of the era combined; this book, whose lens follows the systemic, records that fact here as its standing correction — *contained* never meant *kind*, only *bounded*.² The settlement is not a treaty (nothing this book respects ever is): it is the accumulated procedure — accords, compacts, observatories, corridors — plus the discovery, made independently in three capitals, that the swing bloc's non-alignment is not a phase to be won but a *service to be purchased*: the buffer, the broker, the third clock. Middle powers became load-bearing in 2026, at Islamabad; by 2045 they are the architecture.

**In August**, the solar-system economy produces its first commodity-market casualty, and the century's best joke at hard money's expense. *Karman Metals*' second asteroid-return campaign — robotic, Tianwen-heritage, seven years from tender to splashdown — delivers 44 tonnes of platinum-group metals to a market that consumed them at 2020s prices for catalysis, electronics, and the hydrogen buildout: platinum falls 61% in fourteen months; iridium and palladium follow; three terrestrial mining majors write down their PGM books by Christmas.³ **[MED]** Lunar water, meanwhile, crosses its own quiet threshold — propellant depots at the Lagrange points now fuel the Mars runs and the GEO servicing fleet at a third of Earth-launch cost, the Moon's first export economy, small, real, and non-aligned as Reem's daughter was told.⁴ And the wry callback this book promised its readers holding the other precious metal since Chapter 1: **gold is fine.** Platinum crashed because platinum was always an *industrial* story; gold's two-decade bull ran on a *monetary* one, and no asteroid dilutes a fiscal ledger. Still, the year's flows tell the era's turn: gold closes at $9,800, its first down year since 2026, out-competed at last not by space rocks but by the 1.1% *real* yield on the paper of governments that spent twenty years earning distrust and five earning some of it back. Tomek's mother — eighty-one, Kraków, portfolio long since annuitized — sends her son the year's family verdict, which the notebook preserves next to the platinum chart: *"Sold mine in '34, they laughed. Who is laughing? Nobody. In money, this is called a good century."*⁵

**All year, the third string hums its designed note: AI governance is boring, and boring is what success looks like.** **[HIGH]** The ICA's annual report is a document nobody reads, which is the achievement: 214 registered frontier clusters, 96% attestation coverage, two violations, both fined, neither newsworthy. The accords survive their first great-power stress test in March — a US-bloc senator's leaked memo urging withdrawal; the withdrawal caucus loses 71–28, the same margin, veteran readers will note, by which the bifurcation passed sixteen years ago, populism's arithmetic apparently a constant of the era — and the frontier itself advances at its post-2035 cadence: substantial, supervised, insured.⁶ The alignment community's mid-century retrospectives split between "we over-feared" and "we succeeded and therefore look like we over-feared," an argument this book's audit chapter (2050) will inherit rather than settle. What no one disputes at mid-century: the catastrophic-risk decade everyone braced for arrived as an *insurance market* instead — dependence, not domination, the failure mode that materialized; Brazil, not Skynet, the name on the cautionary tale.

*What the forecast is least sure of from here:* Decade III's premise — that the world now changes more slowly than its inhabitants believe — is itself the wager; the named tails are a compact review crisis (2063, outside this book, but priced by markets within it), the Sail precedent scaling to something less reversible as the 2.0°C years arrive, and the biology arc's second act (the 2054 chapter's mortality claims), on which the author's error bars are widest of all. The interregnum, as this book defined it in 2026, is over: money re-anchored, order settled into procedure, the machines governed, the demographic turn acknowledged. What follows is not resolution but *maintenance* — and maintenance, the last six chapters will argue, is civilization's hardest genre.

> **SIDEBAR: Scoring the 2026 draft, decade two.** *Direction hits:* the biology turn and its pension detonation; cognition-too-cheap-to-meter and the services/asset split; the compact via decentralization; the meaning recession and the revival; peak human recognized in the 2040s; India's rise; tripolar-plus as procedure, not treaty. *Timing slips:* the real-yield re-anchoring ran three years early, and via biology rather than deflation — the spine's stated mechanism was wrong even as its destination held (documented in Ch. 17, kept unsmoothed); the Mars base [LOW] was *granted* by the median path, which the author still regards as the book's luckiest call rather than its best one. *Texture misses, preserved:* Madrid's robotaxis (still Zagreb's decade); the meaning recession's gender crossing was sharper than drafted; nobody in 2026 drafted the *returners*. Grade: direction A−, timing B+, mechanism C+ — the mechanism grade is the honest one, and the far chapters inherit its lesson: the net's knots held better than the author's explanations of *why*. **[Methodology, not forecast]**
