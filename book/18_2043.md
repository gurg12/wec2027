# 2043 — The Worker-less Firm

*Taichung, Taiwan. June 2, 2043.*

Mei-lin Chou retired in March, which means she now works harder for no salary, as her mother predicted with some satisfaction before dying peacefully last winter at ninety-two, treated just enough to enjoy being right. Mei-lin, fifty-four, biological fifty-ish, sits on two standards boards and the district court's new *technical assessors* panel — which is why she has spent four Mondays listening to lawyers argue about whether a company with no employees can be said to have *intent*. The defendant, if that is the word, is a chip-brokerage that has operated for three years out of a Singapore mailbox and a rack of attested compute: it sources, prices, hedges, contracts, and litigates — this case began when it *filed suit* against a Taichung supplier for breach, and the supplier's lawyers responded by challenging its standing to sue at all. The human owners, a Vancouver pension fund, have never attended. The judge asks Mei-lin's panel a question she will repeat at dinners for years: "In your professional judgment, when this system signed the contract, did anything *understand* the contract?" Mei-lin gives the answer thirty years of process engineering trained into her: "Your honor, when my fab signs off a wafer, no single tool understands the wafer. The fab does. The question is not whether something inside understands. It is whether the whole is built so that *someone answers* when it's wrong." The judge writes that down. Mei-lin goes home and tells her old break-room colleague by video that she has spent her retirement explaining fabs to judges, and he laughs and says what everyone her age says now: *of course — the machines were never the hard part.*

---

**In February**, the case the casebooks will simply call *Teleos* arrives at the Delaware Court of Chancery, and the worker-less firm gets its day in the jurisdiction that matters. **[MED]** *In re Teleos Holdings LLC* consolidates three disputes around one uncomfortable fact: Teleos — a freight-and-storage arbitrage operation, human-owned, AI-operated end to end since 2039, no officers, no employees, managers reduced to a quarterly owners' meeting — has for four years signed contracts, taken on debt, been sued, and paid taxes, all through agents acting under a governance charter its owners wrote once and mostly forgot.¹ The vice-chancellor's opinion, 140 pages and instantly canonical, threads the needle this book's whole legal decade hangs from: Delaware law, she writes, has never required that a fiduciary's judgment be *human*, only that it be *attributable* — and existing doctrine (the LLC's contractual freedom, the corporation's own long history as a "legal person" that acts only through instruments) already reaches most of the way.² What the law requires, and what Teleos's charter fails, is an unbroken **chain of answerability**: a named natural person or regulated entity who answers for each class of the system's acts, with authority to halt them. The holding — operate autonomously all you like; *answer* humanly, always — is narrow, technical, and epochal. Within eighteen months, "Teleos charters" are a product; the ICA's attestation rails, built for accords compliance, get a civil-law second career certifying answerability chains; and the case law of digital agency — contract formation, agency costs, respondeat superior for non-human operators, fiduciary duty owed *by* algorithms and *to* owners — begins pouring out of Delaware, Singapore, and London in the one furious decade the spine promised.³ **[MED]**

**In June**, the other shoe is held up, examined, and pointedly not dropped. Legislatures in two jurisdictions — the EU Parliament, on referral from its 2029-vintage AI framework's review clause, and the state of California, via a ballot-initiative-adjacent commission — hold the era's first *serious* hearings on limited AI legal personhood: not rights-talk at the margins, but concrete draft statutes (electronic-person registries, capital requirements, insurance-backed liability caps) modeled frankly on the corporate form.⁴ **[LOW]** Nothing passes. The coalitions against are the story: labor and the churches (personhood as accountability laundering — "a liability shield that dreams"), the insurance industry (unpriceable), and, decisively, the AI industry itself, which learned from *Teleos* that answerability-with-autonomy gives it everything personhood would, minus the culture war. The question is planted, though, exactly as the spine tagged it — planted in committee records, minority reports, and one Strasbourg speech that will be quoted for decades — and this book's far chapters can hear it germinating. The author's honest note: [LOW] here covers "serious debate, no passage"; passage *anywhere* before the 2050s sits below 10% in the July 2026 weighting, and nothing in seventeen years of intervening invention has moved that number much.

> **SIDEBAR: What "worker-less" actually means, statistically.** Teleos is an edge case built for litigation; the economy around it is more interesting. By 2043 roughly 8% of new US business registrations are "thin-crewed" — one to three humans wrapping largely autonomous operations — and the *median* new firm has four employees where 2026's had eight. But full worker-lessness stays rare (~0.4% of firms, concentrated in arbitrage, licensing, and holding structures) for the reason *Teleos* crystallized: answerability doesn't scale to zero. Someone must hold the signature — Tomek's junior engineers learned it in 2041; Delaware codified it in 2043. The deep continuity the punditry misses: the corporation was *always* an artificial person operated through delegated instruments; the 2040s did not invent the worker-less firm, they merely completed a four-century experiment in how few humans the instrument requires — and the answer, it turns out, is *not zero, by law*. **[HIGH on the direction; the percentages are invented]**

**In October**, the year's quieter files, kept for the net. The re-anchoring continues on 2042's new mechanism: the US 10-year real yield touches 0.8%, its highest since the interregnum began, and gold — for the first time in this book — closes a year *flat* ($10,150 against $10,100), its seventeen-year monologue interrupted not by collapse but by competition: paper that finally pays again.⁵ **[MED]** The fertility ledger prints 1.87 global, with the Sweden–Korea package now formally emulated in eleven countries and the first *French* uptick (the demographers refuse to call it that; the newspapers don't). And in the corridor, Corridor Current closes its Series C at a valuation that makes Dele Adeyemi, thirty-five, the kind of rich that requires structures — he spends an irritated week with Vancouver-style lawyers designing the answerability chain for the company's own autonomous trading desk, and emerges with the sentence his engineers print on mugs: *"If it can sign, someone can be woken up at 3 a.m. about it. Otherwise it doesn't sign."* Delaware, it turns out, scans perfectly into Lagos English.

**In December**, Mei-lin files her panel's year-end report, then does something retirement is for: she starts writing the memoir her union of process engineers has been asking for — working title *Yield*, on the theory that every meaningful thing she ever did was measured as a percentage of attempts that survived. The last line of her first chapter, shared with this book's author by the fictional courtesy that governs all its sources, is the year's real holding, better than the vice-chancellor's: *We spent thirty years teaching machines to work without us, and the law spent one furious year confirming that "us" was never the hands. It was the name that answers. It was always the name.*
