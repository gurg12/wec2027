# 2039 — The Unilateral Sky

*Chiang Rai province, Thailand. April 19, 2039.*

Anong Boonmee is sixty-six and has opinions about clouds. She had opinions about them when they were only weather; now that they are also policy, her opinions have an audience, because the adaptation office made her a district commissioner — a title that mostly means younger engineers drive out to argue with her, which she enjoys. This morning's argument is about the sky itself. Her grandnephew shows her the feed on his tablet: ships off the Bay of Bengal, three hundred kilometers of coast away and a world away, throwing seawater mist at the sky to make the clouds a little whiter, a little longer-lived, so the pre-monsoon heat that killed four hundred Bangladeshis last April arrives a degree cooler this April. The boy is outraged on schedule: nobody voted, he says; the monsoon belongs to everyone; what if it moves *our* rain? Anong lets him finish. Then she tells him about 1997, when the water didn't come and the bank took the buffalo; about 2034, when the flood pulse took the Charoen family's paddy and her floating panels rode it out; about what it is to farm downstream of other people's decisions your whole life and call it weather. "Somebody has always been adjusting our sky, child," she says. "Coal did it for two hundred years and never held a press conference. At least these ones publish the data." She is not sure she is right. She is sure the argument has changed owners — it belongs to the drowning now, not the comfortable — and that, she tells the dog on the evening dike walk, is not nothing.

---

**In March**, the sky stops being a commons in theory. At a summit in Dhaka, a bloc grown out of the old V20 climate-vulnerable group — Bangladesh and the Philippines in front; Vietnam, the Maldives, Fiji, and nine others signed; India abstaining in the way that supplies the ports — announces **Project Sail**: a regional marine-cloud-brightening deployment over the Bay of Bengal's pre-monsoon warm pool and, in a second phase, the Coral Triangle's bleaching refugia — seawater aerosol, ship-based, seasonal, *reversible in days*, and undertaken, the communiqué says in its single unforgettable sentence, "under the same authority by which others warmed it."¹ **[MED]** The great powers respond as the spine predicted, which is to say: in sequence. Uproar first — a US-bloc démarche, sanctions language drafted in two committees, a unanimous editorial season about Pandora and precedent. Threats second, hollower by the week, as the legal reviews conclude what everyone already knew: no treaty forbids it, the research programs of the 2020s (the UK's ARIA grants, the university flotillas) normalized the technique at exactly this scale, and the deployers are the states with the *least* to lose from a norms fight and the most bodies to count from another April like last year's.² And then, by autumn, the quiet participation: Chinese monitoring vessels "coincidentally" gridding the deployment zone and sharing data through the WMO; American satellite products appearing, unattributed, in the coalition's verification portal; a Gulf sovereign fund taking a services contract through a Singaporean cutout. Governance arrives after the fact, as it has arrived after every fact in this book: in November the WMO stands up a **Regional Radiation Management Observatory** — monitoring, attribution protocols, a seasonal review board with downstream-state seats (Thailand takes one; Anong's office files the district data) — which is not permission, and is not prohibition, and is exactly the shape the century's governance keeps choosing: *supervised faits accomplis*.³ **[MED on the sequence; HIGH that governance trails deployment]**

> **SIDEBAR: What Sail is and isn't.** Marine cloud brightening seeds low ocean clouds with sea-salt aerosol so they reflect a little more sunlight over a bounded region for a bounded time — it is local, seasonal, and stops working days after the ships stop. It is not stratospheric aerosol injection, the planet-scale, years-persistent intervention the moratorium campaigns of the 2020s mainly feared; Sail's designers chose MCB *because* its reversibility made unilateralism survivable. The honest physics: regional cooling of the warm pool measurably blunts heat extremes and may buy the monsoon's timing some stability, at the cost of genuinely uncertain downstream precipitation shifts — the year-one data will be argued over for a decade (Ch. 22 checks in). The honest politics: once one coalition demonstrates that a $2-billion-a-year program can shave the deadliest weeks off the calendar of 300 million people, the question "who decides" has been answered in practice — whoever can, until supervised. **[Mechanism claims HIGH; efficacy magnitude genuinely uncertain, and the text's restraint here is deliberate]**

**In June**, the machines cross the threshold demography wrote for them fifteen years ago: the **ten-millionth care-class humanoid** ships from the Osaka lines, and eldercare in Japan and Korea is now, in the industry's own reporting, the killer app the whole field reorganized around — precisely as the population pyramids always said it would be, precisely as the founders who spent 2024–2028 promising warehouse ballets did not.⁴ **[HIGH]** The wider fleet — logistics, construction, agriculture, the works — passes fifty million cumulative this year, but the care fleet is where the century's need lives: Japan runs one care unit per 4.1 residents over eighty; Korea per 5.3; the units do the lifting, the turning, the night-watch, the medication ferrying, while the scarce humans — Marisol's profession, choreographers now licensed on four continents — do what the 2030 pilots learned could not be automated: the reassurance, the judgment, the being-known. Marisol herself spends the year commissioning her company's Lisbon academy — three hundred supervisor-trainees a cohort, a quarter of them recruited from Kigali and Manila under the remote-care annexes of 2036 — and gives the graduation speech the trade press reprints under the headline the field has waited two decades for someone to say plainly: *"The robot is the stretcher. You are the medicine."*⁵

**In October**, the strings cross in the year's least-noticed statistic, filed here because this book exists to enlarge fine print: for the first time, global spending on *managing the climate we have* — adaptation, resilience, Sail-class regional interventions, loss-and-damage flows — exceeds global spending on fossil-fuel extraction. The energy transition's accounting identity has inverted; the old economy's capex is now smaller than the weather's invoice.⁶ **[MED]** The same month, the anomaly ledger prints its warning against complacency: 2039 will close at +1.82°C globally — Sail cools a warm pool, not a planet — and the first serious *SAI* proposal since the 2020s moratorium campaigns surfaces, not from a vulnerable coalition this time but from an insurance consortium's white paper, and is shouted down in a week. The window Sail opened has a shape, and the shape is: regional, reversible, supervised — *for now*. The 2047 chapter inherits the "for now."

**In December**, Anong hosts the observatory's district review at the co-op hall, because the provincial office flooded its own car park in September and she has never once let anyone forget it. The year's local data: the pre-monsoon peak came in 1.1 degrees under the counterfactual band; the rains came nine days late but adequate; the co-op's twenty-second year closed with the floating arrays' output up 3% on cooler water, a number she reads aloud twice. The visiting engineers want her verdict for the minutes. She gives them the one this chapter was built to earn: "Write that the sky was one degree kinder, and that we are watching who it thanks." Then she feeds them, because the argument is over and the argument is never over, and that too is governance, the oldest kind: rice, shade, and the keeping of accounts among neighbors.
