# 2042 — When 90 Became 75

*Madrid, Spain. March 14, 2042.*

Marisol Ferreyra is forty-eight, runs the Iberian division of Europe's second-largest care company, and is presiding — there is no other verb — over the strangest staffing meeting of her career. The problem is not the robots, which behave, or the choreographers, whom she trains by the hundred. The problem is the residents: Santa Lucía is *emptying from the young end*. The facility's intake age, seventy-four when she started answering phones there fifteen years ago, is eighty-eight now and rising; the seventy-somethings who once filled the intermediate wing are on senolytic-and-reprogramming protocols, hiking the Sierra on Tuesdays, and — this is today's agenda item — applying to *work* for her, because the company's re-entry program for treated seniors pays well and their pension formulas, indexed in a slower century, no longer stretch. Item four is Dr. Ferrán, seventy-nine, cardiologist, retired 2028, re-certified last spring, biological age per his clinic printout sixty-one: he wants clinical supervisor hours, and Spanish labor law has no category for him. Her deputy proposes calling the cohort "returners." Her scheduling agent proposes, with the tact of its kind, "supply." Marisol, who watched her mother in Rosario work to seventy-eight out of necessity dressed as vigor, writes the real problem on the whiteboard herself, in the blunt Spanish of her profession: *Nos pagaron para cuidar la vejez. La vejez se mudó.* They paid us to care for old age. Old age moved.

---

**In January**, the confirmation arrives with a decade of longitudinal weight behind it: the *NEJM* consortium paper — 41 cohorts, nine countries, treated versus untreated since the approvals of 2036 — establishes **compressed morbidity** as measured fact, not marketing.¹ **[MED]** Treated cohorts at chronological seventy-five carry the disease burden untreated cohorts carry at sixty-six to sixty-eight; healthy life expectancy in the leading rich countries prints at **84 for men, 88 for women** and is rising roughly four months a year; and — the finding that gives the chapter its name — the modal trajectory has changed *shape*: not longer decline, but longer plateau, with mortality compressing into a late, brief window. Ninety is not the new seventy in lifespan terms; seventy-five is the new sixty in *function* terms — "when 90 became 75," the science press's phrasing, technically backwards and culturally exact. The caveats are the politics: treated cohorts skew wealthy, insured, urban, and northern; the untreated majority ages on the old curve; and the gap between the curves is now the developed world's most legible class boundary, visible in gait, in re-employment, in the intake age at Santa Lucía.²

**In April**, the arithmetic detonates where arithmetic always detonates: the pension systems. The trigger is technical — the EU's ageing-report revision incorporating the NEJM curves shows pay-as-you-go liabilities discounted against retirement ages set for the *old* morbidity schedule, a €4.1-trillion present-value hole across the union — but the response is anything but.³ **[MED]** France, faithful to form, produces the spring's iconic images: two million in the streets against "indexation," the retirement of the 62-versus-64 wars of the 2020s replayed at higher stakes and higher ages. Japan, faithful to *its* form, legislates first and gently: the Diet passes **healthspan indexation** in June — pension eligibility tied, by formula, to measured national healthy-life-expectancy, phased, with hardship carve-outs for the untreated and for manual occupations. Korea follows in October; Italy falls to a technocratic decree nobody votes for and everybody obeys; and the fight of the decade is joined on the terms the spine promised: **retirement age indexed to healthspan** — actuarially obvious, politically radioactive, and morally genuinely hard, because the treated-untreated split means indexation without stratification taxes the poor's old age to fund the rich's second youth.⁴ The formula that eventually travels — "index to the *median*, carve out by *occupation and biomarker*, fund the carve-outs with the longevity dividend itself" — is Japanese in origin, Danish in refinement, and gets adopted the way all this book's settlements do: piecemeal, sullenly, after every alternative has been tried in the streets.

> **SIDEBAR: The longevity dividend, priced.** The fiscal panic of 2042 obscures the ledger's other column, which the actuaries publish and the placards ignore: compressed morbidity is *cheap*. The last-two-years-of-life cost spike — always the health system's largest single line — shifts later but does not grow; chronic-disease years per capita fall outright; and the re-entry of treated sixty-and-seventy-somethings into paid and civic work (11% of the OECD's over-65s already, doubling every four years) adds labor supply exactly where demography subtracted it. Run honestly, the numbers say the crisis is *distributional, not existential*: the money exists; it is sitting in the gap between pension formulas written for 1990's bodies and taxes written for 2020's. That gap is a political artifact, and political artifacts, as this book has argued since the Warsh hold, are exactly the things that take a decade of screaming to move. **[HIGH conditional on Ch. 17's premise]**

**In August**, the strings braid: the money string meets the body string, and gold — this book's fiscal thermometer — prints its year of the decade, $9,400 to $10,100 between April and November, as markets digest what healthspan indexation *means*: rich-world sovereigns openly re-writing their largest liabilities, which resolves the long-feared pension cliff not by default but by *renegotiation* — bullish for solvency, corrosive for the sanctity of every other index-linked promise.⁵ **[MED]** The 10-year real yield, 0.3% for half a decade, drifts to 0.5% as the demographic-deflation thesis takes its first data hit: if the old are working, the labor force isn't shrinking on schedule, and the 2040s' promised re-anchoring (this book's own money-string destination) begins arriving — not as a crisis, as a *re-pricing*, the first upward real-yield trend since the interregnum began. The author notes, for the audit, that this is the mechanism the 2026 draft least expected: the money string re-anchors not because demographics turned deflationary as promised, but because biology *un-turned* them. Direction right, mechanism wrong — the forecaster's most common grade, collected here in public.

**In December**, Marisol's year closes the way the chapter's whiteboard demanded. The company launches its *returner* track formally — Dr. Ferrán supervises three wards and mentors eleven choreographers; the union, after two bitter months, writes the first mixed-age-cohort agreement in European care; and Santa Lucía's emptied intermediate wing reopens in November as something the 2026 version of the industry could not have imagined needing: a **transition residence** — not for the dying, for the *re-arriving* — where treated seventy-somethings spend subsidized months figuring out, with coaches from April Sandoval's franchise network and pension counselors fluent in the new formulas, what the found decade is for.⁶ At the opening, a returner asks Marisol the question of the year, the meaning recession's sequel: "They gave us back the time. Who gives us back the *point*?" Marisol — who has been answering versions of that question since a paper list on a Metro ride in 2027 — gives the answer her whole career has been assembling: "Nobody gives it back, señora. We staff for it." It goes on the training-academy wall in Lisbon, under the stretcher line. The 2040s, it turns out, have a curriculum, and it is being written in eldercare homes by the formerly laid-off, which is either irony or justice, and Marisol has stopped distinguishing.
