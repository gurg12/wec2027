# 2036 — The Biology Turn

*Lagos, Nigeria. September 7, 2036.*

Dele Adeyemi is twenty-eight, a founder now — Coastline spun off its grid-software team last year, and he owns eleven percent of a company that balances electricity for nine million people — and this morning he is being aged. The clinic is in Lekki, glass and quiet; the machine reads his blood, his retina, the methylation clock ticking in his cheek cells, and prints the number his generation is learning to carry around like a credit score: biological age 26.1, two years under chronological, "cardio trending excellent, sleep debt material." His grandmother came here too, last month — that is the part that would have been science fiction in the fuel-queue years. Mama Adeyemi is eighty-one; the national eldercare scheme now covers the senolytic course that cleared the stiffness from her knees, one of the first African cohorts, six years after the drugs debuted in Boston and Basel at prices that made them a rich man's rumor. She calls the pills *panadol for old age* and refuses to be impressed. What impresses her: Dele drove her to the clinic through Third Mainland traffic, and the car — electric, Lagos-assembled, self-driving in the corridor lanes — never once made her grip the door handle. "Your father drove that route for thirty years," she says. "It took something from him every year. This thing gives it back." Dele, who buried his father two years ago with diesel still in his lungs, writes the sentence down at the next charging stop. Some inheritances you refuse. Some you wire into everything you build.

**In February**, the decade's opening argument files its first exhibits: the aging-pathway approvals. **[MED]** The EMA moves first — a senolytic combination for osteoarthritis and early cardiac fibrosis, the class's flagship indication since the trials of the late 2020s — and the FDA follows in June with two more, including the first partial-reprogramming therapeutic, a locally delivered optic-nerve treatment restoring function in glaucoma patients, descendant of the epigenetic work that entered clinics in 2026.¹ Nobody approves "aging" — the regulators, having spent a decade refusing that category, approve *diseases* whose common mechanism everyone can read in the label's fine print. The distinction lasts about a month against the market. By autumn, longevity clinics from Zurich to Seoul to Lekki are prescribing on-label medicine with off-label intent, demand curves go vertical among the over-fifties who can pay, and the phrase that will organize the next decade's health politics is already circulating in ministry corridors: **the treated and the untreated** — two populations, one pharmacology apart, aging at measurably different rates before a single ethics commission has scheduled its first hearing.² **[MED]** The ethics catch up the way they always do in this book: after the fact, on a deadline, with the data already embodied in people.

> **SIDEBAR: What the drugs actually do (2036 edition).** No approved therapy in 2036 extends maximum lifespan, and the companies mostly stopped claiming otherwise. What the first generation does is *compress morbidity at the margin*: senolytic courses clear a class of dysfunctional cells implicated in joint, cardiac, and kidney decline (measured effect: function scores equivalent to being 4–7 years younger in treated tissues); reprogramming therapies re-open a narrow window of repair in specific tissues, eyes first because eyes are small, accessible, and immune-privileged. Stack the first generation with GLP-1s — eighteen percent of rich-world adults were already on those by 2026 — plus diagnostics that outperform panels, and the population-level result is not immortality but *slope change*: the average seventy-year-old of 2036 carries the disease burden the average sixty-five-year-old carried in 2020. The pension actuaries noticed before the philosophers did. They always do. **[The mechanism claims are [MED]; the actuarial arithmetic is [HIGH] conditional on them]**

**In April**, the other turn — the one measured in gigatonnes — gets its retroactive announcement. The Global Carbon Project's annual budget, with two years of revised data behind it, formally dates **peak global emissions to 2035**: 38.1 gigatonnes, now declining at just under one percent a year, declared by spreadsheet rather than treaty, exactly as this book's energy string predicted the honor would arrive.³ **[HIGH]** The decomposition is unromantic: China's peak (circa 2025) finally outweighing India's growth; the OECD's accelerating electrification; the tropics leapfrogging combustion entirely on Anong-economics; and the great glut itself — oil at $44, gas structurally cheap — having made *new* fossil infrastructure unfinanceable years before demand followed. Nobody celebrates, correctly: the stock, not the flow, sets the temperature, and the stock is still growing. But the era in which the emissions curve's *sign* was civilization's referendum ends this year, quietly, in a dataset — and the fight moves fully to the two fronts that will own the rest of the book: adaptation, and the awkward question a coalition of drowning states is about to force about the sky (Chapter 14 keeps that promise).

**In August**, the rent swap completes its ledger entry: a Financial Times tabulation confirms that Gulf sovereign funds now sit among the **top five holders of global compute capacity** — the block outside Karachi, the Abu Dhabi campuses, tolling stakes in three hyperscalers' Middle Eastern regions, and, since March, an anchor position in the fab consortium building the Gulf's first leading-edge node.⁴ **[MED]** Reem al-Otaibi's employer, in other words, has finished becoming what its 2026 annual report promised: a fund that converted the twentieth century's rent into the twenty-first's. The strategic geometry deserves the paragraph the newspapers give it: the states that once controlled the marginal barrel now control a meaningful slice of the marginal *thought*, and they price it the same way — long contracts, swing capacity, studied neutrality between the blocs. OPEC's true successor was never a cartel of electrons, as the 2020s guessed; it is a cartel of *sites* — sun, land, capital, and permission concentrated where objection is not a growth industry.

**In October**, the year's two turns meet in a Lagos conference hall, at the African Union's first continental longevity-and-workforce summit — a pairing that sounds like satire and is actually arithmetic: the continent whose median age is twenty is negotiating labor mobility treaties with continents whose treated seventy-year-olds now work by choice.⁵ **[MED]** The summit's leaked actuarial annex is the year's most-shared document among people who share actuarial annexes: if morbidity compression holds, the aging world's care-labor demand *falls* on a fifteen-year lag even as its longevity rises — which would strand the sending-country training pipelines built in the 2033 treaties. The African delegations, holding the only young workforce on Earth and newly literate in its price, negotiate accordingly: shorter treaty terms, portability clauses, and — the Lagos declaration's real innovation — *remote-care provisions*, under which a nurse in Kigali supervises care robots in Osaka without leaving the time zone her family lives in. Marisol Ferreyra's licensure category, invented in Spain in 2032, turns out to scale across oceans. The future of work, the summit's rapporteur writes in the year's most quoted sentence, *is not where the worker is; it is where the responsibility lands.*

**In December**, the ledger takes the year's marks: gold $8,300, oil $44, US CPI 2.7%, the 10-year real yield at 0.3% — the money string's suppression now so established that a whole generation of portfolio managers has never priced duration any other way — and, in the fine print that this book exists to enlarge, the first annual *decline* in global oil demand outside a recession: 99.4 million barrels a day, down 800,000, the peak now visibly behind at 2033 and no one able to agree on which quarter.⁶ The century's two greatest curves — carbon up, people up — have now both bent within eighteen months of each other, and almost nobody marked either day, because the days only exist in retrospect. Tomek's notebook, volume sixteen, closes the year with the entry his 2056 self will photograph for this book's last chapter: *Emissions peaked last year. Oil peaked three years ago. Nobody threw a party. Biggest things move slowest and arrive quietest. Note for the forecast: the loud years were the small ones.*
