# 2038 — The Taiwan Compact

*Taichung, Taiwan. March 30, 2038.*

Mei-lin Chou watches the announcement in the fab's break room, which is where she watched the drill ten years ago, and the difference she registers first is somatic: nobody has stopped eating. On the screen, in Singapore, three delegations that officially never negotiated with each other read three separate statements that officially have nothing to do with one another, and the sum of the three — no unification moves, no independence declaration, the strait's air and sea protocols demilitarized to coast-guard rules, a standing hotline, everything reviewable in twenty-five years — is the thing her whole professional life has been priced against, dissolving into procedure at a podium. Her colleagues debate lunch. A process engineer half her age asks, sincerely, what the big deal is; the strait has been boring since before he interned. Mei-lin, fifty this year, senior enough that her badge opens doors she no longer visits, finds she has to sit down for a moment with her tea. She thinks of Phoenix — the fab they built in case her island burned; the marriage that didn't survive the building of it — and of her mother saying *they built ours there so neither burns*, and of how offended she'd been by the sentence's cold arithmetic, and how the arithmetic had then simply, over ten years, come true. The young engineer is still waiting for an answer. "The big deal," she says, "is that you don't think it's one. We paid thirty years for your shrug." She means it as a rebuke. It comes out, to her surprise, as a blessing.

---

**In March**, the compact. **[MED]** Formally there is no compact — there are *the Singapore Understandings*, three coordinated unilateral declarations (Beijing's, Taipei's, Washington's) plus a technical annex on strait protocols negotiated by coast guards, the diplomatic equivalent of a treaty wearing three raincoats — but the world files it under the name the headlines choose, and this book follows the world.¹ The terms are the status quo, formalized: no unification, no independence, demilitarized ADIZ and median-line protocols, verification by the same attestation-and-monitoring culture the accords normalized, and a 25-year review that everyone involved describes, privately, as "a mortgage on the question." What made it possible is precisely what a decade of anxious op-eds said was impossible to wait for: the island's *strategic deflation*. Leading-edge capacity 54% and falling, the chip chokepoint decentralized by the very panic the 2028 drill induced, the mainland's demographic-industrial complex needing the strait's trade more than its symbolism, Zhao's new administration needing an early, cheap, world-historical win — and an American bloc that discovered, post-accords, that it preferred managing a rival to mobilizing against one.² The island is no longer a single point of failure; *that* is why nobody has to fail over it. The residual risk does not go to zero — this book's own July 2026 notes put roughly one-in-five weight on a coercion path that no compact forecloses, and that number survives the signing, written into the annex's silences.³ But the modal path gets a signature — or rather, three carefully non-adjacent ones.

> **SIDEBAR: Why chips had to get boring first.** The 2020s consensus held that Taiwan's semiconductor monopoly was its "silicon shield" — too valuable to attack. The 2030s revealed the darker corollary: too valuable to *stabilize*. A single point of failure is a standing temptation to everyone — to coerce it, to seize it, to preempt its seizure. Decentralization inverted the logic on every side at once: Phoenix, Kumamoto, Dresden, and the Gulf node made blockade a bounded harm rather than a global hostage-taking, which lowered both the coercion's payoff and the panic that made accidents likely. The general principle, which this book has now watched govern oil (2026), compute (2035), and silicon (2038): *chokepoints don't get defended into safety; they get diversified into irrelevance, and the diplomacy signs afterward.* **[HIGH as a pattern claim]**

**In June**, the year's other mainstreaming: the brain-computer interface count crosses **half a million implanted patients** worldwide, and the technology completes its decade-long walk from miracle to Medicare line item.⁴ **[MED]** The composition tells the story the 2026 headlines never predicted: paralysis and ALS communication, the founding indications, are now barely a fifth of the fleet; auditory restoration is a quarter; and the majority indication, since the pivotal trials of 2033–35, is **treatment-resistant depression and OCD** — closed-loop stimulation with response rates the drug era never touched, which is what moved the insurers, which is what moved the volume. Two systems dominate — one American, one Chinese, mutually unreadable by regulation and attestation both — and the surgical robot does the placement in forty minutes through a two-millimeter port. Meanwhile the *elective* frontier stays exactly where the spine predicted: fringe and slightly disreputable. The year's emblematic scandal is a Singapore fund's "cognitive throughput program" — attention-modulation implants for its traders, eleven volunteers, two lawsuits, one memoir — which accomplishes for elective BCIs what Theranos once did for blood tests: a decade of guilt by association that the medical fleet, pointedly, does not share.⁵ The line the era draws is legible in April Sandoval's Ohio and Mei-lin's break room alike: repair is *covered*; enhancement is *suspect*. It will hold, this book wagers, until repair's definition finishes eating the distinction — the 2054 chapter inherits that problem.

**In September**, the strings braid quietly in the background of a year the headlines gave to Singapore. The accords survive their first attestation scandal — a registered Gulf cluster caught running an unregistered training exercise through a telemetry gap; the ICA's response (a fine, a firmware revocation, and publication of the method) is studied in every capital and pronounced, with relief, *boring*.⁶ **[MED]** The care-class humanoid fleet passes eight million units, two-thirds of them in East Asia, the Osaka supervisor shortage now a Korean and Italian shortage too. And the fertility ledger prints its now-annual undershoot — global TFR 1.95 — with one bright anomaly the demographers descend upon: Sweden and Korea both print *rises* for a third consecutive year, the first data anyone can plausibly attribute to the purpose-economy package — four-day weeks, third-place funding, housing cost relief in the reconstruction-linked building boom — rather than to bonuses that never worked.⁷ It is two countries, three years, and every caveat applies. But it is the first time in this book that the fertility string bends *up* anywhere for a legible reason, and the 2044 chapter will need the precedent.

*Saskatoon, October 16.* Tomek marries Amrita Chandra on a cold bright Saturday — she is a hydrologist; they met professionally, arguing about a reservoir — and the wedding is small, four-day-week casual, with his grandmother attending as a hologram from Kraków that his cousins keep repositioning toward the cake. The notebook, volume eighteen, gets one line that weekend, written on the hotel stationery Amrita will frame years later as the most romantic thing he never said aloud: *Compact signed. BCIs 500k. TFR 1.95. Married A. — the only series I track that only goes up.* Then, because he is Tomek, a second line, smaller: *Reservoir argument: she was right. Note for the forecast: marry your peer reviewers.*
