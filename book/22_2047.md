# 2047 — Two Degrees, Rehearsed

*Chiang Rai province, Thailand. April 24, 2047.*

Anong Boonmee is seventy-five, treated just enough to garden fiercely, and has outlived two governments' worth of engineers who told her what the water would do. This April is the hottest ever measured in the north — the first of the world's +2.0 years arriving, as she puts it, *personally* — and the district she has commissioned for eight years is executing the drill it has rehearsed for five: the school day inverted to dawn and dusk; the cooling halls (third-place grants, retrofitted with the co-op's own storage) open around the clock; the reservoir's floating arrays trimmed to shade the fish; the elderly census walked house to house by the care units and the service-corps kids, because the heat kills alphabetically — alone, old, upstairs — and lists defeat it. Her grandnephew, twenty-six now, an adaptation engineer with the provincial office (the argument at the tablet in 2039 having become, as arguments with Anong tend to, a career), runs the drill from the co-op hall. Anong's job is the part no dashboard holds: she sits with Mrs. Chansiri, ninety-one, who will not leave the house her husband built, until the not-leaving becomes a negotiated staying — sensor, neighbor, water jar, the eldest granddaughter's number taped by the door. At dusk the district posts its numbers: heat admissions four, deaths zero, load peak shaved 31%. Her grandnephew wants her verdict for the minutes, as ever. "Write that we practiced," she says. "Weather forgives the practiced. Write that, and then come eat before the mangoes are all for the birds."

---

**In January**, the number stops being a threshold and becomes an address. The WMO's consolidated series confirms it mid-month: 2047 will run above **+2.0°C** for the calendar year — the first, and the decade's projections show such years arriving *regularly* now, one in three by the 2050s, the long-run trajectory still bending toward the 2.2–2.4°C corridor by century's end that the post-peak emissions math (falling 1–2% annually since 2035) has implied for a decade.¹ **[HIGH]** The discourse does what this book's thresholds always do: nothing, loudly. The two-degree line spent forty years as civilization's red line, and its crossing is a Tuesday — because the line's real work was done in the decades when it moved capital, and by 2047 that work is either finished or priced. What remains is the century's actual climate politics, and the year supplies its two registers: the *rehearsed* — Anong's April, replicated across ten thousand districts, adaptation now a licensed profession with 11 million practitioners, curricula, malpractice insurance, and a union — and the *unrehearsed*, which is the year's darker ledger: the Indus flood sequence displaces nine million across two monsoons, and the difference between Sindh's toll and Thailand's is not virtue but *balance sheets* — the adaptation-finance flows of the 2030s went where creditworthiness lived, and the gap this book flagged in 2026 (needs 12–14× flows) closed to threefold in the rich tropics and barely halved in the poorest basins.² **[HIGH on both registers]**

**In March**, managed retreat completes its migration from euphemism to municipal line item. **[HIGH]** The year's exhibit is not a drowned village but a *budget*: the OECD's survey counts 3,100 jurisdictions with active relocation programs — buyouts, receiving-district construction, the "second address" arrangements pioneered on the Carolina coast and the Vistula delta — administered now with the affect of school-zoning: contested, procedural, survivable.³ The politics matured the way everything in this book matures: through actuaries. Insurance retreated first (the 2030s' non-renewal waves), lending followed, and the state arrived last with the checkbook and the maps, converting an unspeakable word into a mortgage product. The receiving geography is the quiet revolution — the century's internal migrations flow *toward* the rehearsed districts, the cooling-hall towns, the corridor cities that planned for arrival — and 2047's real-estate data prints the century's new gradient plainly: elevation and preparation now price like school districts did in 2010.

> **SIDEBAR: Sail at eight, and the ledger of the sky.** The Bay of Bengal deployment enters its ninth season this year, and the observatory's decadal review — downstream seats and all — publishes the reckoning the 2039 uproar demanded. Findings: pre-monsoon heat mortality in the program zone down an estimated 40–60% against counterfactual; monsoon-timing effects detectable, regionally mixed, and — the honest line — *within the model disagreement band*, meaning eight years of data cannot yet convict or acquit the clouds of moving anyone's rain. Two more coalitions have followed (the Coral Triangle's second phase; a Gulf-of-Guinea feasibility consortium with Corridor money in it), both regional, both reversible, both supervised — and the SAI question, planet-scale and persistent, remains where 2039 left it: proposed annually, declined annually, the moratorium-by-habit holding precisely because the regional programs give the desperate a governed alternative. The author's 2026 weighting on *ungoverned* SAI-by-2050 was ~15%; the intervening years have not moved it. **[MED, and the "for now" of Ch. 14 still stands]**

**In August**, the profession gets its portrait, and this book keeps a promise made in a Madrid dateline twenty years ago. Marisol Ferreyra, seventy-three— no. Marisol is fifty-three; it is Sr. Ibarra who would have been a hundred and nine, and the author leaves the error and its correction in the text as a monument to the ledger discipline this book lives by.⁴ Marisol, fifty-three, takes the year's strangest commission of her career: the care company's climate division — heat is now an eldercare problem before it is anything else, and the choreographer's license added a thermal module in 2044 — seconds her to Seville for the summer as commissioning director of Andalusia's cooling-hall network, where the skills turn out to be identical: the robots handle logistics, the humans handle *thresholds* — who will not leave, who cannot ask, who needs the negotiated staying. Her August report to the region, leaked because it deserved to be, is the profession's credo in four sentences, and the reason this chapter carries her name: *"Adaptation is not infrastructure. Infrastructure is the easy 80%. Adaptation is a woman who will not leave her husband's house, and a system humble enough to work around her dignity. Fund the last 20% or bury the first 80% with her."*

**In December**, the year closes at +2.04°C, deaths from the April heat across the rehearsed districts a fraction of 2041's toll at lower temperatures, the Indus displaced still, in their majority, displaced — both facts true, neither canceling the other, the century's climate ledger now permanently double-entry. Anong's mango tree, for the record her grandnephew appends to the minutes against all protocol, produced despite everything; the birds got most of them; she called it a fair tax on a kind year, which it was not, and which is why the sentence survives — somebody in every rehearsed district, the minutes' readers understood, has to be the one who keeps calling the years kind, or the practice stops being worth the practicing.
