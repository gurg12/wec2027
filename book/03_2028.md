# 2028 — The First AI Election

*Taichung, Taiwan. October 9, 2028.*

Mei-lin Chou has worked in semiconductor fabs for thirteen years, long enough to remember when the machines needed her more than she needed them, and she is spending the fourth morning of the quarantine doing what everyone in Taiwan is doing: acting normal, competitively. The mainland calls it a "customs enhancement exercise" — every vessel bound for the island boarded for inspection, politely, paperwork in triplicate, no shots fired, the sea lanes turned into a queue. Her fab has 61 days of every critical input except one photoresist precursor, which has 19. She knows this because she sits on the continuity committee now; everyone senior enough sits on some committee. Her mother calls from Kaohsiung to ask if she should buy rice. Mei-lin says the supermarkets are fine, which is true, and doesn't say *the precursor has 19 days*, which is also true. On the news, a spokesman she has watched for years says the island has weathered worse, and the anchor nods, and the whole performance is so calm that it takes her until evening to name what's different this time: the foreign customers aren't calling to ask when shipments resume. They're calling to ask about her *team* — who might relocate, what Phoenix pays, whether her process module can be stood up in Arizona or Kumamoto by Q3. The island's protection was always that it was irreplaceable. She spends her overtime hours, these nine strange days, helping customers rehearse a world where it is not — and being paid triple for it, and hating it, and doing it well.

**In March**, the threshold crosses in the least cinematic way possible: a product update. **[MED]** One of the two leading American labs ships an enterprise tier whose agents retain what they learn — across sessions, across weeks, across the specific sludge of a particular company's codebase and vendor list and org chart — and *improve on the job*, the way a hire does.¹ The launch post is 900 words and uses the phrase "persistent workplace memory" instead of any of the phrases that would move Congress. Within a quarter the industry term of art is **resident agents**: you don't prompt them, you *onboard* them. The disbelief is loud and instructive — the benchmark crowd points out, correctly, that scores barely moved; the skeptic press runs "AI's Memory Trick Is Mostly Hype" columns even as the six-month-retention cohort data inside deploying firms shows error rates falling 40% with tenure.² The capability that matters most this decade arrives, in other words, exactly as the 2027 crash predicted: quietly, jaggedly, and on the enterprise side of the wall, invisible to every consumer and legible to every CFO. By December, resident-agent seats are the fastest-growing line item in enterprise software history, and the class-of-2027 problem has a mechanism as well as a statistic: the agent doesn't just do the junior's tasks — it *accumulates the junior's experience*.

> **THE OTHER ROAD — Branch Point One: The Jump. (Author's split: ~70% the gradual path this book follows / ~30% this box.)**
> In the other world, it isn't a product update. In the other world, one lab's internal automated-research loop closes cleanly in the spring of 2028 — models designing their successors' training runs with enough autonomy that capability begins compounding on a monthly rather than yearly cadence, the METR horizon curve kinks visibly upward, and within two quarters the leading lab holds something it plausibly cannot see the edges of.³ Secrecy fails in weeks; there is always a memo, and there is always a photograph of the memo. Washington, mid-election, does not deliberate — it *commandeers*: emergency compute authorities, a lab-consolidation order drafted in a weekend, export controls turned to embargo. Beijing, reading consolidation as sprint, sprints. The safety teams get everything they asked for — budgets, board seats, a seat in the Situation Room — and none of what they wanted, because the race dynamic they spent a decade warning about is now the explicit policy of both superpowers, with the 2028 election refought overnight as a wartime plebiscite on who can be trusted to run faster. Down this road, the 2030s this book describes — bifurcated regulation, a boring compute treaty, medicine thawing the backlash — never get the chance to be boring. I weight it ~30%: the mechanism is real (recursive self-improvement is the explicit roadmap of every frontier lab), but the 2026–27 evidence — horizon doublings arriving on schedule but *reliability* lagging, integration friction eating each capability's economic punch for years — argues that the compounding runs through the sociotechnical mud rather than around it. If I am wrong here, I am wrong about everything after this page, and Appendix B says so in bold.

**In June**, the campaign discovers the issue has no other side of the aisle to hide on. The primaries settled it: every surviving candidacy in both parties ran against *something* about the machines — the bills, the layoffs, the landlords of compute — and the general election becomes a contest over which grievance owns the frame.⁴ **[HIGH]** The Republican ticket, anchored by the sitting vice president, runs on the administration's record — the war won, the strait open, the border closed — and on a promise that AI will be "American, and America's": accelerate the buildout, preempt the states, tax nothing. The Democratic nominee — **Dana Whitfield**, 52, two-term governor of Michigan, a former utility engineer who spent 2027 becoming nationally famous for forcing a data-center developer to fund a ratepayer rebate — runs the other way: *the machines pay for the transition.* Her stump math is four sentences long: compute pays a levy; the levy funds wage insurance and the grid; consumer AI gets a label; enterprise AI gets audited like a bank. Affordability plus AI-anxiety plus electricity bills — the fusion first visible in the 2026 midterms — turns out to be one issue wearing three coats, and Whitfield's engineer-populism wears it best.⁵

**In September**, the campaign's information environment crosses its own threshold: the first cycle in which a majority of political media impressions are synthetic. **[HIGH]** Not deepfakes of candidates, mostly — the platforms police those with reasonable success — but the ambient tide: generated local-news sites (an election-season census finds 1,200 of them), generated door-knock scripts, generated constituents mailing generated grievances, twelve thousand micro-targeted variants of the same ad, each seen by no journalist.⁶ The week the census publishes, the two campaigns do something unprecedented and coordinated: both begin cryptographically signing all official video at capture. It is the first time provenance appears in American life as a *competitive necessity* rather than a standards document, and the engineers running the signing infrastructure will spend 2034 explaining that this was the moment the architecture was actually decided. **[MED]**

**In October**, the drill. For nine days — October 6 through 14 — Beijing conducts the "customs enhancement exercise" that Mei-lin Chou spends on the continuity committee: a quarantine-in-all-but-name, every Taiwan-bound vessel boarded and inspected, executed entirely with coast guard and customs vessels, announced as temporary, and timed, no one doubts, to the American election. **[LOW]** The scare stays a scare. Washington moves two carrier groups and — the load-bearing move — says almost nothing in public while the back-channel works; Tokyo and Seoul quietly refuse basing escalations; the exercise ends "as scheduled" on day nine, a schedule no one had previously seen.⁷ Markets, though, complete the lesson they began learning in Hormuz: the TAIEX drops 18% and recovers half; memory and logic spot prices spike 60% and *don't* fully recover; every board on Earth with silicon exposure orders the same study by Christmas, and the study says: *diversify the island's throughput by 2032 or write it into the risk factors.* The chip decentralization that will make 2038's compact possible starts here, as a line item — and Mei-lin's inbox fills with Phoenix.⁸

**In November**, Whitfield wins: 51.6% of the two-party vote, 312 electoral votes, the House held, the Senate flipped by one. **[MED — the direction of the winning coalition, not the name]** The exit polls make it the first American presidential election in which "technology/automation" outranks "the economy in general" as a stated top issue — 24% of voters, plurality among under-35s, where her margin is 22 points.⁹ Two footnotes to the returns will matter more than most of the returns: the compute levy polls *better* among Republicans than the preemption plank does, which every 2030s legislator will remember; and turnout among 22–29-year-olds hits a modern record, the missing-rung cohort voting like a class for the first time. The concession is gracious; the transition memos, leaked in December, are not — the outgoing administration's own agencies estimate the buildout's grid draw was under-forecast by a third.

**In December**, gold closes at $6,090 — new highs, quietly, on no war at all. **[MED]** The bull case has rotated: not Hormuz, not the drill, but the arithmetic visible in the President-elect's first budget conversations — deficits at 7% of GDP into the 2030s under every scenario in the CBO's fan chart, a Fed at 3% with inflation at 3, and the world's central banks still absorbing sixty-odd tonnes a month as reserve insurance.¹⁰ The 2026 spike was fear. The 2028 price is *policy*. On the year's last trading day, the 10-year TIPS real yield prints 0.9%, down 40 basis points in eighteen months while nominal growth ran hot — the interregnum's money-string signature, now unmistakable to anyone tracking it in a notebook.

*Saskatoon, December 22.* Tomek graduates a semester early into the missing rung and steps around it: the utilities are the one employer whose demand curve bends the other way. Saskatchewan's grid operator hires him — twenty-two, iron ring on his little finger — into an interconnection group drowning in applications: solar co-ops, two data centers, a potash electrification project, all queued behind transformer lead times running four years.¹¹ His job, described honestly, is triage of the future. On his last day home before the holidays he shows his mother the gold chart — she is, finally, up 13% on the March 2026 tranche — and she asks him whether that means things are getting better or worse. He starts three different answers, abandons each, and finally gives the one his notebook has been teaching him all along: *it means everyone else is asking your question, Mama, and paying to avoid the answer.*
