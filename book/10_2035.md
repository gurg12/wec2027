# 2035 — The Compute Accords

*Kharkiv, Ukraine. May 8, 2035.*

Oksana Melnyk runs tower cranes, which means that for nine hours a day she has the best view in Kharkiv of the argument between what happened and what's next. From the cab of crane 6 — Sofia, the crews name them, always women, always saints or grandmothers — she can see the memorial district they left as it fell, the new machine-tool works the Koreans financed, the university tower with its double-skin facade going up plate by plate beneath her hook, and, on clear days, the line itself, forty kilometers north, where her brother spent three years and where the monitors' white drones still stitch the sky morning and evening, regular as prayer. She was a schoolteacher in 2022. She is a better crane operator than she was a teacher, she says, because children forgive you and steel does not. Her wages have doubled twice since 2029; her crew is Ukrainian, Polish, Vietnamese, and — since last spring, nobody having made it a ceremony — two quiet Russians from Belgorod with work permits and the specific carefulness of men who know they are a headline waiting to happen. The foreman's tablet schedules the pours around the grid's solar peak; the reinforcement cages arrive pre-tied by a plant robot; Sofia's anti-sway is machine-assisted, though Oksana keeps her hand on the joystick anyway, out of respect. At the shift's end she rides the man-lift down through the smell of cured concrete, which is, she has decided, the smell of the argument being won: not justice — nobody up here confuses construction with justice — but *continuation*, poured floor by floor, with her name on the crane log.

**In March**, the diplomats stop pretending the Brazil file is closed and start pretending they always intended what comes next — the great-power reflex this book has learned to read as progress. The G20's post-event working group, nine months of technical sessions deep, publishes the framework that November will sign in Geneva: registration of all compute clusters above a training-capability threshold; on-die attestation telemetry — the hardware reports what class of workload it runs, cryptographically, by construction — manufactured into every accelerator sold by the participating supply chains; and an inspectorate, the **International Computation Agency**, with the right to physical audit of registered sites.¹ **[MED]** The design principle is stated in the preamble with unusual candor: *verification via hardware, not trust* — the IAEA's lesson transposed, because atoms and flops share the property that the dangerous quantities are countable if you control the supply chain, and by 2035, three fabs' worth of chokepoints still do.

**In November**, the **Geneva Accords on Advanced Computation** are signed by the US-led bloc, the China-led bloc, and 31 of the swing states — India and the Gulf in, after extracting technology-transfer annexes priced precisely at their leverage; a rump of non-signers (and everyone's intelligence services, everyone assumes) remaining outside.² **[MED]** The accords do not touch open weights — that argument was lost and won in the 2020s — they attach *liability* to deployment scale instead, which is why they could be signed at all. The commentary divides on schedule: the safety community calls it a floor built five years late; the accelerationists call it a ceiling built five years early; the historians note that this is precisely what every arms-control regime has been called on signing day, and that the regimes so described averaged four decades of imperfect, load-bearing service. What nobody disputes: eleven days in Brazil accomplished what fifteen years of white papers could not. Governance, in this book's world as in the last century's, is built after the fact, out of the fact's rubble.

**In July**, fusion arrives, politely, into an energy economy that no longer needs saving. The ARC pilot in Virginia holds net electric gain for a continuous 30-day campaign — 190 megawatts to the grid, Q_plant just above 1.4 — and a Hefei tokamak matches the milestone within the quarter, to the visible relief of two governments that each preferred not to explain trailing.³ **[MED]** The papers are historic; the power-purchase agreements are merciful; the joke writes itself in every energy newsletter the same week: *fusion arrived just in time to be unnecessary.* Solar-plus-storage, at $38/kWh packs and installation rates above 1.4 terawatts a year, has already won noon everywhere and evening at every latitude that matters; what fusion gets is what the joke concedes — industrial heat, winter firming at high latitudes, and the strategic comfort of a dense source no weather can embargo. A first commercial tranche — fourteen units, mostly Nordic, Korean, and Canadian — books for the 2040s. It is a triumph, and it is a *niche*, and the century's energy historians will explain at length why those were never opposites.

**All year, Ukraine compounds.** Seven consecutive years now above 6% growth; the EU accession protocol initialed in September with a 2038 target date; Kharkiv and Lviv the continent's two fastest-growing metros; the reconstruction complex — Polish logistics, Korean industrial plant, Turkish contracting, Ukrainian software eating all of it — now measurably Europe's growth engine, worth 0.4 points of EU GDP growth all by itself.⁴ **[HIGH]** The line, meanwhile, has completed its transformation into the thing nobody signs and everybody prices: monitored, mined, touristed at its safer ends, crossed daily by permits and remittances, Korea's armistice replayed at European scale — a peace made of procedure, renewed every November by a mandate vote that has become as liturgical as the drones. Moscow under Kolesnikov's consolidating regency spends the year doing exactly what the cynical-normalization thesis predicted: selling verification access under the accords it signed early (it needed the fab-adjacent carve-outs), swapping prisoners on schedule, and prosecuting the war's chroniclers faster than its profiteers. Nobody is satisfied. Everything holds. **[HIGH]**

*Taichung, October 12.* Mei-lin Chou comes home after six years in Phoenix — two fabs stood up, one marriage quietly concluded, a desert she never learned to love — to run process integration at the Taichung mega-site, and finds the island she left mid-scare living something subtler than safety: *redundancy*. The 2032 diversification deadline came and went; the island now holds 54% of leading-edge capacity instead of 90%, and the strange gift of mattering less is visible everywhere — fewer carrier transits in the strait, more mainland tourists, a defense budget that stopped doubling.⁵ Her mother, eighty-five now, asks over dinner whether Phoenix was the future. Mei-lin considers the question professionally. "Phoenix is *a* future," she says. "They built ours there in case ours burned. Now both exist, so neither burns." Her mother, who lived through the decades when the island's safety was its irreplaceability, finds this either profound or backwards and says so, and they argue about it pleasantly until the tea is cold, two process engineers of different eras, debugging deterrence.
