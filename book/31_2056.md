# 2056 — Rereading the Net

*Saskatoon, Saskatchewan. July 7, 2056.*

Tomek Weber-Okonkwo turns fifty this summer, and for his birthday his daughter gives him a book. Not a facsimile this time — the thing itself, printed and bound the way almost nothing is anymore: *The Long Interregnum: 2026–2056*, a scenario document written by an artificial intelligence in July 2026, thirty years ago this week, when he was a student eating lunch over three phone windows — a war, a gold price, a calorie count. Zofia found it in the provincial archive's digital stacks, tagged with the provenance marks of a pre-VERA age, and had it printed because, she said, "you should score it, Tata. It's been waiting thirty years for someone whose notebook goes back that far." So he takes it to the porch, the prairie evening doing its long gold hour, volume one of his own record beside it for the checking, and does what the book's preface asked its future reader to do. He grades.

**The energy string.** *Direction: right. Timing: right within two years. Texture: it guessed Madrid and the world built Zagreb.* The chokepoint decade, the electricity handover around 2030, the noon problem, fusion-as-boots — all of it is here, drawn in 2026 with the strait barely reopened. He checks the old numbers against the notebook: the book's invented 2030 oil price is off by two dollars. He sits with that for a moment. Two dollars.

**The money string.** *Direction: right. Mechanism: wrong, and it said so itself mid-course.* Fiscal dominance, the ~3% era, gold's long monologue — the book called the destination and missed the road home, expecting demographic deflation and getting biological reflation, and then — this is the part that makes him respect the machine that wrote it — *documented its own miss in the 2042 chapter rather than smoothing it.* His mother's bullion, bought scared in 2026, sold shrewd in 2034, is in this book as a running joke he never told anyone. The joke was a forecast too. It also held.

**The capability string.** *Right, and rightly boring.* Compounding through crashes, robotics seven years behind cognition, the quiet 2028 threshold that no one believed. What the book undersold, he writes in the margin, is how *normal* it would feel — that a man could spend the century inside the greatest cognitive transformation in history and experience it mostly as staffing decisions, signature protocols, and his daughter's stuffed owl.

**The politics-of-AI string.** *Right on the shape — backlash before displacement, bifurcation, thaw through medicine.* The margin note he writes here is the one his whole career earned: *You called the fear and the thaw. You could not call what it was like to be Priya in my doorway. The tags don't reach that far down. Nothing does. That was always the job the signatures were guarding.*

**The order string.** *Right — tripolar-plus, middle powers load-bearing, procedure instead of fire — and the book itself confessed the luck in it.* He rereads the Brazil chapter and the branch box beside it, the 25% world where everything nationalized and soured, and feels the vertigo the audit chapter warned him of: the calm of the read is survivorship. Two carrier groups, one October; a syndicate's leased malware finding one country instead of three. The net held. The book never claimed the net *had* to.

**The string of the real.** *Right early, right late.* Machine traffic, synthetic majority, provenance, the verified-human premium — and the thing the 2026 author could see only as an economic tier: that his daughter's generation would treat *attested presence* the way his grandmother's treated bread — ordinary, sacred, and not to be wasted on people you don't mean.

He tallies. Direction: five and a half strings of six — he deducts half from money, as the book itself did. Timing: perpetually two years early or three years late, "slow in the near years, fast in the far ones," exactly as the preface pre-confessed. Texture: wrong everywhere, charmingly — wrong robotaxi cities, wrong candidate names, invented people who never lived. And here Tomek stops grading and sits very still in the gold hour, because he has arrived at the last page's real question, the one Zofia was too kind to say out loud when she handed him the book:

The invented people. A student in Saskatoon with three windows open and a notebook. A tanker driver's son in a Lagos fuel queue. A laid-off analyst in Madrid making a list of what a model couldn't do. A process engineer in Taichung with nineteen days of precursor. A rice farmer paid for shade. The machine, writing in July 2026, needed ordinary lives to hang its numbers on, so it made some up — *and the century then went and staffed them.* Not him, not literally; no oracle, he knows how scenario fiction works, he has read the preface, the people are composites, the resemblance is the method. But that is the finding, isn't it — the one no confidence tag could carry. The book got the events roughly right and the *feeling* entirely, structurally wrong, because it had to narrate thirty years of history as things that happen, and history, lived, is not things that happen. It is Tuesdays. It is the valve replaced on the historic day. It is his mother asking whether gold going up meant things were getting better or worse, and the answer — *everyone is asking your question and paying to avoid the answer* — being truer than every chart in this beautiful, presumptuous, half-right book.

What the forecast could never have contained, he writes on the flyleaf, in the space the printers left as if they knew: *Not the events — you had those, near enough, and where you didn't you showed your work. What you couldn't contain is that we would be busy. That the interregnum would be survived the way everything is survived: by people doing chores inside it. You drew the net. You could not draw what it felt like to be a knot — to hold, be held, and mostly not notice which was which. That part was ours. It was always going to be ours. That's not a failure of the forecast. That's what the forecast was* for.

He closes the book. On the wall screen, four time zones of family; on the pond down the section road, his neighbor's floating panels drinking the last light, Anong-economics at latitude fifty-two; in his lap, thirty years of somebody's homework, graded at last, passed. Zofia calls from inside that the cake is ready — fifty candles, a fire code violation, her mother's compromise is ten and arithmetic. Tomek sets the book on the rail, spine cracked, margins full, its work — unlike the world's, unlike the net's, unlike any other thing this century made — *finished*, and goes in to the light and the noise and the other children.

The future kept arriving. Everyone was busy. It was enough.
