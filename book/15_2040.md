# 2040 — Everywhere and Nowhere

*Youngstown, Ohio. May 22, 2040.*

Tanya Krupa's father lost the last good job in 2008 and spent the next twenty years explaining whose fault it was; Tanya, forty-four, got the new good job eighteen months ago and has stopped trying to explain it to him, because the explanation offends every rule he organized his grief around. The plant — heat pumps and grid transformers, the century's two most backordered machines — sits on the bones of a mill her grandfather worked, and it did not come back for the labor. Labor is 6% of unit cost; there are 240 humans on a floor that would have needed 2,600 in her grandfather's arithmetic, and Tanya is one of them because someone must hold the licenses, walk the exceptions, and sign — her actual title is exception supervisor, which her father read on her badge and pronounced, correctly, "foreman for robots." The plant came back for the substation next door — interconnection rights her employer bought before the press release, four hundred megawatts of the Lake Erie offshore build — and for the customers, eleven hundred miles of grid buildout in every direction, and for the tax abatement, which is eternal America. Her father asks who the plant belongs to. A Korean chaebol, a Gulf fund, an Ontario pension: she shows him the ownership chart once and he studies it like a ransom note. "In my day you knew who owned the mill," he says. "Yeah," she says, "and he lived in Palm Beach and closed it anyway. Mine lives in a spreadsheet and keeps the lights on. I'll take the spreadsheet." She doesn't fully mean it. She fully takes the paycheck.

---

**In March**, the trade economists publish the decade's confession: the reshoring wars of the 2020s ended, and *everyone* won, which is to say the question dissolved. **[MED]** With direct labor collapsed toward single digits of manufactured cost — the humanoid fleet at seventy million, the resident agents running the schedules, the lights-out night shift a solved default — production has stopped chasing wages, because wages stopped mattering, and has redistributed toward the only inputs that still vary: **energy and demand**.¹ The factory returns to Ohio *and* Lagos simultaneously, exactly as the spine promised, and for the same reason: cheap electrons plus adjacent customers beat cheap hands plus a container voyage, now that hands are capital goods. The map this produces is stranger than either side's 2020s rhetoric — Youngstown transformer lines and Coral-corridor battery plants; Guangdong running lights-out for a shrinking home market; Germany making machines for Kharkiv's boom; Mexico and Vietnam, the great labor-arbitrage winners of the last cycle, scrambling up-stack as the arbitrage evaporates beneath them.² Trade in *goods* flattens; trade in *designs, electrons, and licenses* explodes. "Everywhere and nowhere," the FT's series calls manufacturing now — everywhere as capacity, nowhere as a place your cousin can walk in without a license. Tanya's 240 badges are the whole settlement in miniature.

**In June**, the consensus machine finally stamps the story this book has been carrying since Dele stood in a fuel queue doing physics: **Africa's demographic dividend is the growth story of the decade** — no longer the frontier-fund pitch, but the base case in the IMF's flagship, the sell side's allocations, and, most tellingly, the mobility-treaty renegotiations, where the sending continent's terms harden yearly.³ **[HIGH]** The numbers that moved the consensus: the continent's working-age population up 190 million since 2026 while every other region's shrank or stalled; the AI-tutored cohorts of the early 2030s now entering a labor market where the global price of expertise is two cents and the scarce input is *young humans who can hold responsibility* — which is to say, the one commodity Africa has in structural surplus. And the geography of it concentrates exactly where the spine pointed: the **Lagos–Abidjan corridor**, six hundred kilometers of coastal city-system stitched by the completed highway, the standard-gauge line, and Dele's industry — 51 million people, the century's Pearl River Delta, growing a Portugal every four years.⁴ Dele Adeyemi, thirty-two, spends the year co-founding the thing his whole biography was rehearsal for: **Corridor Current**, a joint venture merging his grid-software firm with an Abidjan logistics platform — power scheduling, customs pre-clearance, and cold-chain routing sold as one product, because in the corridor they *are* one problem. His grandmother, eighty-five now and pharmaceutically annoyed about it, attends the signing in Accra by hologram and delivers the benediction the trade press quotes for years: "My husband carried cocoa to a ship he never saw again. My grandson ships electricity to people he video-calls. Same ocean. Better manners."

**In September**, the species establishes its first permanent address on another planet, eleven people at a time. **[LOW]** *Meridiani Base* — the consortium flags say more than the name: SpaceX's ships, NASA's life support, Japanese and Emirati modules, an Indian relay constellation — is declared "continuously crewed" when the second crew's overlap with the first makes the settlement's population never-zero, a definition lawyers argued about for a year and the eleven residents do not care about at all.⁵ It is small, fragile, scientifically thrilling, and economically symbolic, precisely as tagged: the base produces water, oxygen, and data, consumes a budget with nine zeros, and returns, in its first year, the only export that was ever really scheduled — meaning. An eight-year-old in Chiang Rai, in Youngstown, in the corridor, can look up in 2040 and know there are lights on two worlds. The engineers among this book's readers are asked to note the tag and forgive the poetry; the [LOW] is doing honest work (the July 2026 dossier had Starship still mid-development and every Mars schedule in history running late), and the base's fragility is a plot fact the 2052 chapter will collect.

**In December**, the year's ledger closes a half-decade the 2020s would not have believed for its *quietness*: no systemic crisis since Brazil, the accords boring, the compact holding, growth's five-year average the best since the 1960s — and the era's discontents accordingly migrating inward, to the pension actuary's table and the empty nursery and the purpose economy's waiting lists, where the next five chapters live. The State of the Net below marks the halfway point of this book's thirty years. Its author, from the vantage of July 2026, notes what the table cannot: every number in it was once a fight, and the century's strange achievement so far is how many of the fights ended in *procedure* — accords, compacts, observatories, exception supervisors — rather than in the fire the 2020s priced. The net, at mid-book, is holding. What it is holding *up* — that is Decade II's remaining question, and it is not an economic one.
