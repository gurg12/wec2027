# 2026 — The Year the Strait Closed

*Saskatoon, Saskatchewan. August 14, 2026.*

Tomasz Weber-Okonkwo — Tomek to everyone except his Polish grandmother — is twenty years old and eats lunch with his phone propped against a ketchup bottle. Three windows are open. The first is a liveblog counting down the Islamabad Memorandum's sixty-day window, which closes in two days; the second is a gold chart, because his mother converted a quarter of her retirement savings to bullion in March, near the top, and the family now tracks the price the way other families track a sports team in decline; the third is a calorie counter, because he is trying to put on muscle before field season. He is a third-year environmental engineering student. In February he watched a war start on this phone; in March he watched the price of diesel in a landlocked prairie city respond, within nine days, to a naval blockade on the far side of the planet. His thermodynamics professor called it "the most expensive lesson in systems coupling ever taught." Tomek has started keeping a notebook of numbers — oil, gold, wheat, the temperature anomaly — with no plan for it except the vague conviction that someone should be writing these down in one place. Around him the cafeteria argues about whether the ceasefire will hold. He checks the calorie counter. He is 340 short. He gets the second sandwich. Whatever happens on Sunday, field season starts Monday.

---

The year's first half already belongs to the history books, so this account begins where the books stop: July.¹ The war that opened on February 28 with roughly nine hundred strikes in twelve hours — killing Iran's Supreme Leader on the first morning — and closed the Strait of Hormuz over most of thirteen million barrels a day, the largest disruption the oil market has ever suffered, has decayed into something stranger than war and lesser than peace.² Brent, which touched the $120s in March, sits back at $72, as if the whole thing had been a rumor.³ Iran's new Supreme Leader, Mojtaba Khamenei, has now held the office four months without once appearing in public; his father was buried in early July — a four-month wartime delay Tehran never explained — and the son's absence from the funeral cameras did more to unsettle Tehran than the eulogies did to steady it. The Islamabad Memorandum — signed June 17, brokered not by a great power but by Pakistan — set a sixty-day clock for a final framework. All summer the clock is the market.

**In August**, the clock nearly runs out. **[MED]** The Doha working groups deadlock over sequencing — Iran wants a sanctions tranche *before* an enrichment freeze; Washington wants verification first — and for ten days in early August tanker insurers quietly reprice war risk in the Gulf; Brent grinds from $71 to $84 while officials say the word "progress" in four languages. The window closes on August 16 with no deal. It reopens forty-one hours later with a partial one: the **Doha Annex**, announced August 18 — an enrichment freeze at reactor grade under returning IAEA monitors, traded against the release of an $18 billion tranche of frozen assets and a 120-day extension for everything else: the permanent Hormuz protocols, the remaining sanctions architecture, the stockpile itself.⁴ Nobody calls it peace. Diplomats call it "the annex" and traders call it "the fade," because that is what the risk premium does, all autumn, a little more each week. A cold peace begins the way cold peaces do: as a schedule of meetings nobody cancels.

> **SIDEBAR: How a war produced a glut.** The strangest chart of 2026 is oil's round trip — a full Hormuz closure to $70 crude in four months. The mechanics: the blockade forced the largest simultaneous drawdown of strategic reserves ever attempted, accelerated Asian import diversification that will never fully reverse, and pushed OPEC+ — minus the UAE, which walked out of the cartel mid-war and never came back — to keep unwinding production curbs into the recovery to defend market share. Demand did its part: the price spike itself destroyed some, and the structural erosion from electrified transport quietly deepened all year. By July the IEA's balances showed the market oversupplied through 2027. The lesson traders internalized — *chokepoint risk is a spike, not a plateau* — will price every crisis of the next decade, which is roughly how long it will take to stop being true.⁵

**In September**, the Federal Reserve does nothing, spectacularly. **[MED]** Kevin Warsh — confirmed 54–45 in May, the most contested Fed chairmanship in the institution's history — walks into his third FOMC meeting with headline CPI at 4.3%, nine of his colleagues on record leaning toward hikes, and futures markets pricing a coin flip.⁶ He holds at 3.50–3.75% and says the quiet logic aloud: the inflation is energy pass-through from a strait that is now open, and the fastest-acting disinflation program in the world is the glut. The Fed, he tells the press conference in substance, will not chase a supply shock up the mountain and down again — and then, because he is Warsh, he re-anchors hawkishly: no cuts either, not while wages run at 3.5%. It is the first time markets meet the doctrine that will define his chairmanship: *hold everything, promise nothing, let oil do the work.*

**In October**, gold decides the hold was dovish. **[MED]** The metal, $4,000 in the summer trough after its January madness — $5,589 intraday on the 28th, a 29% single month, then a correction of more than a quarter — begins climbing again on a simpler thesis than war: a Fed that won't hike into 4% inflation, a Treasury issuing $2 trillion a year, and central banks still buying roughly sixty tonnes a month as if de-dollarization were a subscription service.⁷ By Halloween it clears $4,600. Tomek's mother stops checking the price with an air of apology. The 10-year Treasury, meanwhile, refuses to rally the way textbooks say it should when growth is this soft — closing October at 4.4% — and a few analysts start using, carefully, in client notes, the term that will own the next decade: *fiscal dominance.*

**In November**, the United States votes about the future while arguing about bills. **[HIGH]** The midterms were supposed to be about the war; they turn out to be about affordability — groceries, rents, and above all **electricity**, up double digits in utility territories adjacent to data-center clusters. Democrats take the House by 14 seats; the Senate stays Republican by one. But the signal is inside the results: by one count, 61 winners across both parties ran advertising explicitly against AI, data centers, or both — the moratorium candidates outperformed their districts' baselines by 3 points, and the pattern held in red exurbs as in blue college towns.⁸ It is the first American election where "who owns the machines" polls as an economic issue rather than a technology one. The morning after, three governors announce data-center siting reviews. Heatmap's tracker, which counted 20 project cancellations worth $41.7 billion in the first quarter alone, will end the year past 70.⁹ The industry's response is already visible in permits: the buildout moves — to Alberta, to the Gulf states, to northern Sweden, to anywhere with cold air, cheap electrons, and no ballot initiative. Compute, like water, flows around the rock. **[HIGH]**

**Also in November**, half a world away, a quieter number: Waymo crosses 800,000 paid rides a week and opens its London depot; Amazon's warehouse robot fleet passes 1.1 million, within rounding error of its human workforce. Nobody protests a warehouse robot. The backlash has a shape, and the shape is *visibility*.¹⁰

**In December**, the labor data goes soft enough to hear. **[MED]** November payrolls print −8,000 — the first negative month since the pandemic — with the losses concentrated where the year's whole story has been hiding: professional and business services, information, finance. The entry-level numbers are worse; job postings tagged "analyst, 0–2 years" run 31% below 2024. At the December meeting Warsh holds again but retires the hiking bias, and futures move to price three cuts by mid-2027. Oil obliges the doves: Brent breaks $60 in mid-December and closes the year at $61, in the middle of the "fifties-to-sixties" corridor the glut analysts have been promising.³ Gold ends at $4,680. The S&P, which spent the year being carried by eleven AI-adjacent stocks, ends up 4% — a number that flatters a market where the median stock fell.

**And in the east, the winter war peaks.** Russia opens the cold season with its largest barrage yet against Ukraine's grid; Ukraine answers the way it has all year — downstream, at the refineries. By New Year's the running count of Ukrainian deep strikes on Russian refining passes 300 for the year, holding 13–17% of Russian throughput offline at any given time, with the Gazprom Neft Moscow refinery — 40% of the capital's fuel — dark until at least spring.¹¹ Sixty Russian regions ration gasoline. The ruble's managed float gets more managed. In late December the central bank quietly folds two mid-sized banks into VTB over a weekend, the internet gets slower and more Russian, and the Finance Ministry's liquid rainy-day fund — 6.5% of GDP before 2022 — ends the year functionally empty.¹² Nothing collapses. That is the point the year insists on, in Russia as in Tehran as at the Fed: 2026 is the year systems learned to run degraded. **[HIGH]**

*Lagos, Nigeria. December 28.* Dele Adeyemi, eighteen, spent March in a fuel queue holding his father's place from 4 a.m., doing physics problems by phone-light — the blockade reached Apapa Road as a 71% diesel spike, and his father's tanker sat idle for five weeks. In December the queues are gone and the generator drones again, but something else has happened on his street: the pharmacy, the barbershop, and the church have all put panels on their roofs this year, because after March nobody trusts diesel and the Chinese modules have never been cheaper.¹³ Dele has been reading about microgrids instead of medicine. His father says medicine. The panels say otherwise. He applies for both and tells neither parent.

The year ends with the strait open, the war it named unfinished everywhere else, and a set of numbers — $61 oil, $4,680 gold, a 4.2% ten-year, 3.8% inflation, −8,000 jobs — that don't add up to either of the futures loudly on offer. In the gap between those futures, mostly unreported, the quieter series compile: 665 gigawatts of solar installed worldwide this year; token consumption at the AI labs up roughly threefold even as their valuations wobble; 705,809 babies born in Japan, fifteen years ahead of the projection's schedule.¹⁴ Tomek, home for the holidays, copies the year's last entries into his notebook and starts a second page for 2027. He has learned one thing this year, he writes, and it isn't about oil: *everything is coupled, and the coupling is the story.*
