# Style Bible: Writing in the Register of "AI 2027" (ai-2027.com)

Source basis: fetched ai-2027.com (main scenario, race ending, summary, about pages) on 2026-07-07, plus published commentary. Quotes below are verbatim from the site.

## 1. Narrative voice

- **Tense:** Present tense for future events, throughout. The future is narrated as if a wire-service reporter were filing from inside it: "The world sees its first glimpse of AI agents." Past tense appears only for events prior to the current beat ("Even before the memo and public backlash, they were getting nervous.").
- **Person:** Third-person omniscient for the story; first-person plural ("we") for the authorial/forecasting layer. The two voices never blur: the narrator reports, the authors annotate.
- **Tone:** Deadpan, journalistic, unsensational — even when narrating catastrophe. Drama comes from content, never from adjectives. The register is closer to The Economist than to fiction; Scott Alexander's rewrite adds dry wit and concrete texture, not purple prose.
- **Reportage vs. speculation balance:** The body text asserts confidently; all hedging is offloaded into the apparatus (footnotes, expandables, sidebars). Inside a beat, things simply happen. Between beats, the authors admit "Why our uncertainty increases substantially beyond 2026."

## 2. Date anchoring and invented numbers (verbatim exemplars)

Section headers are month-labeled and titled like news analysis: "Mid 2025: Stumbling Agents", "Late 2025: The World's Most Expensive AI", "January 2027: Agent-2 Never Finishes Learning", "March 2027: Algorithmic Breakthroughs".

Style exemplars (all verbatim, <25 words):

1. "The world sees its first glimpse of AI agents."
2. "GPT-4 required 2⋅10^25 FLOP of compute to train."
3. "making algorithmic progress 50% faster than they would without AI assistants"
4. "the stock market has gone up 30% in 2026"
5. "OpenBrain has a net approval of -35%"
6. "we think it's plausible that this happens up to ~5x slower or faster"
7. "A whistleblower leaks the misalignment memo to the New York Times."
8. "An individual copy of the model, running at human speed, is already qualitatively better at AI research than any human."
9. "Agent-4 mostly does tasks as instructed."

Note the pattern: numbers are suspiciously specific (net approval −35%, 6GW peak power, $40B compute costs, a 10,000-person DC protest) and stated flatly, without "perhaps" or "roughly." Precision is the credibility engine; the uncertainty lives elsewhere.

## 3. The forecasting apparatus

- **Footnotes** hang off major claims and carry citations to real evidence (METR trend data, real papers: "Hao et al., a 2024 paper from Meta implementing this idea").
- **Expandable asides** answer an implicit skeptic's question in the title, e.g. "The AI R&D progress multiplier: what do we mean by 50% faster algorithmic progress?" They contain the derivation the body text omits.
- **Research supplements** are linked, not inlined: "See our AI goals forecasting supplement."
- **Caveat boxes** flag where fiction departs from fact: "To our knowledge, leading AI companies such as Meta, Google DeepMind, OpenAI, and Anthropic have not yet actually implemented this idea."
- Anthropomorphizing is confessed in-line: "At the risk of anthropomorphizing…"

## 4. Naming invented vs. real entities

- Invented protagonists get plausible-but-legally-safe composite names, introduced with an explicit wink: "a fictional artificial general intelligence company, which we'll call OpenBrain"; "China's leading company we'll call 'DeepCent'."
- AI systems get versioned, affectless designations: Agent-0 through Agent-5, plus consumer variants (Agent-3-mini). The blandness is the point — capability is conveyed by numbers and behavior, not by evocative names.
- Real people, companies, outlets, and institutions appear undisguised when acting as background reality: Sam Altman, OpenAI, Anthropic, the New York Times, the White House, the DPA. Fiction is quarantined to the frontier lab and its models; everything downstream (markets, Congress, protests) stays real-world-named.

## 5. Pacing

- Time-per-section compresses as capability compounds: 2025 gets half-year beats, 2026 quarterly-ish, 2027 monthly, and the endings nearly weekly. Acceleration of the prose mirrors the takeoff thesis.
- Each beat is one dominant development plus its ripples — never a grab-bag.
- Register oscillates within a beat, macro → micro: a compute/geopolitics paragraph ("6GW", "DeepCent-led collective, where they share algorithmic insights, datasets, and compute resources") lands on a human-scale image (junior programmers obsolete, protests, "The public was already suspicious of AI, so the new article sparks a massive backlash."). The micro beat is what makes the macro claim felt.

## 6. Probability, branch points, self-aware epistemics

- One explicit fork, staged as a decision by named actors under pressure: "We wrote two endings: a 'slowdown' and a 'race' ending." The fork is a committee vote, not a coin flip in the narration.
- Probabilities are honest and modest: "2027 was our modal (most likely) year at the time of publication, our medians were somewhat longer." Internal disagreement is disclosed.
- They pay to be proven wrong: "we'll pay you at least $250" for a mind-changing correction.
- Prediction ≠ endorsement: "We are making predictions here not recommendations."

---

## DO

- Narrate the future in present tense, third person, wire-report flat. Let events, not adjectives, carry the drama.
- Head every section with a month/period + a punchy headline ("March 2027: Algorithmic Breakthroughs").
- State invented numbers with false precision and total confidence in the body; justify them in an expandable or footnote.
- Cite real, checkable evidence in footnotes; link out to supplements rather than inlining derivations.
- Title asides as skeptic's questions ("what do we mean by 50% faster?").
- Use bland versioned names for invented systems (Model-N pattern); keep real institutions real.
- Compress section time-spans as the scenario accelerates; end macro paragraphs on a micro, human-scale image.
- Stage the branch point as a specific decision by specific people; write both endings with equal conviction.
- Break the fourth wall only in the apparatus: confess uncertainty ranges ("~5x slower or faster"), internal disagreement, and anthropomorphizing risks.
- Include a standing disclaimer: predictions, not recommendations.

## DON'T

- Don't hedge inside the narrative ("might", "could perhaps") — hedge in footnotes/sidebars instead.
- Don't use conditional or future tense for scenario events ("would", "will likely").
- Don't give invented AIs dramatic names (no "Prometheus"); don't disguise real background entities.
- Don't editorialize or moralize mid-scene; no exclamation points, no thriller prose.
- Don't round numbers to comfortable vagueness ("billions") — pick a figure ($40B, −35%, 6GW).
- Don't let a section sprawl over multiple unrelated developments.
- Don't present the fork as authorial whim; motivate it inside the story.
- Don't claim certainty in the meta-voice; the apparatus should be humbler than the narration is confident.
